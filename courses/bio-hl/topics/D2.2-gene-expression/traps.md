# D2.2 Gene expression — where the marks go

## Treating gene expression as DNA replication

**Commonly written:** “A gene is expressed when the cell copies its DNA.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** DNA replication copies the genome for cell division. Gene expression uses genetic information to produce a functional RNA or protein.

**Scores instead:** “A protein-coding gene is expressed when it’s transcribed into mRNA and translated into a polypeptide whose function can affect phenotype.”

## Calling promoters and transcription factors the same kind of molecule

**Commonly written:** “The promoter is a protein that binds to the gene and starts transcription.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A promoter is a DNA sequence, while transcription factors are proteins that bind specific regulatory DNA sequences.

**Scores instead:** “Transcription factors bind regulatory DNA, and transcription machinery assembles at the promoter to begin transcription.”

## Reversing the effect of mRNA stability

**Commonly written:** “Rapid mRNA degradation produces more protein because the mRNA is processed faster.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Degradation ends that mRNA molecule’s availability for translation, so rapid breakdown usually reduces the amount of protein produced.

**Scores instead:** “A stable mRNA can be translated repeatedly, while rapid degradation shortens the time available for translation.”

## Giving differentiated cells different genomes

**Commonly written:** “A muscle cell and a neuron work differently because they contain different genes.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The cells generally share the same genome. Their different transcriptomes and proteomes result from selective gene expression.

**Scores instead:** “Muscle cells and neurons express different sets of genes, producing different proteins and specialized functions from largely the same genome.”

## Saying all methylation switches genes off

**Commonly written:** “Adding methyl groups always stops transcription.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Promoter DNA methylation represses transcription, but histone methylation can activate or repress transcription depending on the site modified.

**Scores instead:** “Methylation of cytosine in a promoter represses transcription, while histone methylation can either activate or repress it.”

## Calling an epigenetic change a mutation

**Commonly written:** “Air pollution mutates the promoter by adding methyl groups to its bases.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A methyl tag changes regulation without changing the promoter’s nucleotide sequence, so it’s an epigenetic modification rather than a mutation.

**Scores instead:** “Air pollution can alter DNA methylation patterns and therefore gene expression without altering the DNA base sequence.”

## Claiming twin studies prove an environmental cause

**Commonly written:** “Any difference between identical twins must have been caused by their environment.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Similar genomes reduce genetic variation, but twins can differ in prenatal conditions and other uncontrolled exposures, so an association alone doesn’t prove causation.

**Scores instead:** “Differences between differently exposed monozygotic twins provide evidence of environmental effects, although the study can’t establish a specific cause by itself.”

## Making lactose bind directly to DNA

**Commonly written:** “Lactose binds the operator and switches on the lac genes.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The inducer binds the repressor protein and changes its shape, preventing the repressor from blocking the operator.

**Scores instead:** “When lactose is available, a lactose-derived inducer binds the repressor, allowing transcription of genes needed for lactose use.”
