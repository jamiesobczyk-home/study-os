# B3.1 Gas exchange — where the marks go

## Treating breathing and gas exchange as the same process

**Commonly written:** “Gas exchange is when air moves in and out of the lungs.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Moving air is ventilation. Gas exchange is diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar-capillary surface.

**Scores instead:** “Ventilation refreshes alveolar air, while gas exchange occurs when oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across the alveolar and capillary walls.”

## Reversing the pressure change during inhalation

**Commonly written:** “The lungs expand, so pressure inside them increases and air enters.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Increasing thoracic volume lowers lung pressure. Air enters only when lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure.

**Scores instead:** “Contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles increases thoracic volume, lowering lung pressure so air moves inward.”

## Saying that blood flow creates the gas-exchange surface

**Commonly written:** “Capillaries make the alveoli have a large surface area.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Numerous alveoli provide the large air surface. Capillary beds provide blood contact and maintain concentration gradients through continuous flow.

**Scores instead:** “Numerous alveoli provide a large surface area, while extensive capillary beds maintain steep diffusion gradients by carrying gases away.”

## Calling surfactant a source of moisture

**Commonly written:** “Surfactant keeps alveoli wet so gases can dissolve.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The alveolar lining is already moist. Surfactant reduces its surface tension, helping prevent alveoli from collapsing.

**Scores instead:** “Pulmonary surfactant reduces surface tension in the moist alveolar lining and helps keep alveoli open during exhalation.”

## Saying stomata exist to prevent transpiration

**Commonly written:** “Stomata prevent water loss from the leaf.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Open stomata allow gas exchange but also provide the main route for water-vapor loss. Guard cells regulate that loss by changing pore width.

**Scores instead:** “Stomata allow carbon dioxide uptake, while guard cells regulate pore width to balance gas exchange against water loss.”

## Reporting a stomatal count as a density

**Commonly written:** “There were 18 stomata, so the stomatal density was 18.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Density requires a count per unit area. A raw count can’t be compared when fields of view have different areas.

**Scores instead:** “Stomatal density equals the mean number of stomata counted divided by the area examined, reported as stomata per square millimeter.”

## Explaining the Bohr shift as oxygen being used up

**Commonly written:** “Haemoglobin releases oxygen because the muscle has used its oxygen.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Low oxygen can favor unloading, but the Bohr shift specifically describes reduced haemoglobin affinity caused by increased carbon dioxide and lower pH.

**Scores instead:** “Increased carbon dioxide and lower pH reduce haemoglobin’s oxygen affinity, shifting the curve right and increasing oxygen release.”

## Reversing the foetal haemoglobin curve

**Commonly written:** “Foetal haemoglobin is shifted right because it needs to release more oxygen.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Foetal haemoglobin has higher oxygen affinity, so its curve lies left of the adult curve and it can load oxygen from maternal blood.

**Scores instead:** “The foetal haemoglobin curve lies to the left because foetal haemoglobin binds oxygen more strongly at the same oxygen partial pressure.”
