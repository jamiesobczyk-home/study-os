# D4.2 Stability and change — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Explain the requirements for ecosystem stability. **[4]**

Explain four conditions that allow a natural ecosystem to remain stable over long periods.

### Mark scheme

- A continuing supply of energy is required because energy is lost from the ecosystem as heat. **[1]**
- Nutrients must be recycled / returned to forms available to producers. **[1]**
- Genetic diversity increases the likelihood that populations can survive environmental change. **[1]**
- Climatic variables must remain within the tolerance ranges of the organisms present. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “A stable ecosystem needs biodiversity” earns one mark at most. Full marks require all four distinct requirements and a reason each contributes to stability._

## Q2. Explain an Amazon rainforest tipping point. **[4]**

Explain how continued deforestation could produce a self-reinforcing change in the Amazon rainforest.

### Mark scheme

- Rainforest trees release atmospheric water vapor by transpiration. **[1]**
- Water vapor from a sufficiently large forest contributes to cooling, air flow and regional rainfall. **[1]**
- Deforestation reduces transpiration and rainfall / causes hotter and drier conditions. **[1]**
- Drought, fire or tree death causes further forest loss, producing positive feedback; Accept: the threshold forest area is uncertain. **[1]**

_Examiner note: an answer that stops at “fewer trees means less rain” misses the feedback. Full marks require the return path from drying to further forest loss._

## Q3. Explain eutrophication caused by fertilizer leaching. **[5]**

Explain how nitrates and phosphates leached from farmland can cause the death of fish in a lake.

### Mark scheme

- Nitrates and phosphates enter the lake by leaching / runoff and increase nutrient availability. **[1]**
- Algae or other producers grow rapidly / form a bloom. **[1]**
- Shading and subsequent death of producers increase the amount of dead organic matter. **[1]**
- Decomposer microorganisms respire aerobically, increasing biochemical oxygen demand and reducing dissolved oxygen. **[1]**
- Hypoxia / anoxia causes fish and other aerobic organisms to die. **[1]**

_Examiner note: fertilizers don’t usually remove the oxygen directly. The missing mark in near-full answers is often the aerobic respiration of decomposers._

## Q4. Distinguish bioaccumulation from biomagnification. **[4]**

Distinguish the two processes and explain why DDT or mercury can reach a high concentration in a top predator.

### Mark scheme

- Bioaccumulation is an increase in pollutant concentration within one organism over time. **[1]**
- Accumulation occurs when uptake exceeds metabolism / excretion. **[1]**
- Biomagnification is an increase in pollutant concentration at successively higher trophic levels. **[1]**
- A predator consumes contaminated biomass from many prey, causing a persistent pollutant such as DDT / mercury to reach a high tissue concentration. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “toxins build up in food chains” doesn’t distinguish the terms. The answer must separate change within one organism from change between trophic levels._

## Q5. Assess the sustainability of resource harvesting. **[5]**

A fishery’s annual catch remains constant while surveys show a falling spawning-stock biomass and fewer older fish. Assess whether the harvest is sustainable and outline appropriate responses.

### Mark scheme

- Sustainability requires the harvesting rate to remain below the replacement rate. **[1]**
- A falling spawning-stock biomass indicates that removal is exceeding replacement / future reproduction is at risk. **[1]**
- Fewer older fish may reduce reproductive output / indicates a change in age structure caused by harvesting. **[1]**
- A constant catch alone isn’t evidence of sustainability because fishing effort may have increased / the stock may still be declining. **[1]**
- Accept: reduce quotas / introduce size limits / seasonal closures / protected areas and continue population monitoring. **[1]**

_Examiner note: the trap is treating an unchanged catch as an unchanged population. The survey evidence concerns replacement and therefore carries more weight._

## Q6. Compare primary succession, a climax community and arrested succession. **[6]**

Explain how a terrestrial community can develop from bare rock, then distinguish a climax community from succession arrested by livestock grazing.

### Mark scheme

- Primary succession begins on a bare surface with no developed soil. **[1]**
- Pioneer organisms contribute to weathering and the accumulation of organic matter / soil formation. **[1]**
- Larger plants replace earlier communities as soil depth, water retention or nutrient availability increases. **[1]**
- Primary production, species diversity, food-web complexity and nutrient cycling generally increase during succession. **[1]**
- A climax community is relatively stable and is associated with the prevailing environmental conditions. **[1]**
- Repeated livestock grazing removes seedlings / prevents woodland development and maintains an arrested community such as grassland. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming pioneers, grasses and trees gives a sequence but not the mechanism. Full marks require soil change and a clear reason grazing prevents the expected climax community._
