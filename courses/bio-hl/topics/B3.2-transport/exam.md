# B3.2 Transport — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn't recalled and didn't score.

## Q1. Compare and contrast an artery, a vein and a capillary. **[6]**

Compare their structures and relate each vessel to its function.

### Mark scheme

- Arteries have thicker walls relative to lumen diameter than veins / contain more smooth muscle and elastic tissue. **[1]**
- Arteries have relatively narrow lumens and withstand blood at high pressure. **[1]**
- Veins have thinner, more flexible walls and relatively wide lumens for blood at low pressure. **[1]**
- Veins contain valves that prevent backflow. **[1]**
- Capillary walls consist of a single endothelial layer / provide a short diffusion distance. **[1]**
- Capillaries have narrow lumens and form highly branched networks, facilitating exchange with tissues. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming three vessels and listing unrelated features won't earn comparison marks. Each feature must identify the vessel and, where requested, connect structure to function._

## Q2. Explain how water can move from roots to the leaves of a tall plant. **[6]**

Include the source of the force and the adaptations of xylem.

### Mark scheme

- Water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls and diffuses through stomata / transpiration occurs. **[1]**
- Water loss draws water through leaf cell walls by capillary action and out of the xylem. **[1]**
- Removal of water produces tension / negative pressure in xylem. **[1]**
- Cohesion between water molecules transmits tension through a continuous water column. **[1]**
- Lignified xylem walls resist collapse under negative pressure. **[1]**
- Xylem vessels have a hollow lumen and absent/perforated end walls, reducing resistance to flow. **[1]**

_Examiner note: an answer that says "cohesion pulls water upward" misses the origin of the force. Evaporation creates tension, while cohesion transmits it._

## Q3. Explain the formation, exchange and drainage of tissue fluid. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- High hydrostatic pressure at the arterial end of capillaries forces fluid through the capillary wall. **[1]**
- Water and small solutes leave, while blood cells and most plasma proteins remain in the blood. **[1]**
- Oxygen/nutrients move from tissue fluid into cells and carbon dioxide/wastes move from cells into tissue fluid. **[1]**
- Capillary hydrostatic pressure decreases along the capillary bed. **[1]**
- Retained plasma proteins create an osmotic pull that causes some water to re-enter near the venous end. **[1]**
- Excess tissue fluid enters lymph vessels and is eventually returned to veins near the heart. **[1]**

_Examiner note: "tissue fluid diffuses out" is too vague and gives the wrong mechanism for bulk fluid movement. Hydrostatic pressure causes filtration; individual solutes then cross membranes by appropriate transport processes._

## Q4. Compare and contrast the circulation of a bony fish with that of a mammal. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Both systems use a heart to generate pressure and blood vessels to transport blood. **[1]**
- In bony fish, blood follows heart → gills → body → heart / passes through the heart once per circuit. **[1]**
- In mammals, blood follows heart → lungs → heart → body → heart / passes through the heart twice per circuit. **[1]**
- Blood pressure falls as blood passes through fish gill capillaries, so systemic flow continues at relatively low pressure. **[1]**
- Mammalian blood is re-pressurized after the lungs, allowing lower pulmonary pressure and higher systemic pressure. **[1]**

_Examiner note: counting heart chambers doesn't answer the command term by itself. Full marks require routes through the exchange organs and the resulting pressure difference._

## Q5. Explain how pressure changes and electrical conduction coordinate one cardiac cycle. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- The sinoatrial node initiates an electrical signal that spreads across the atria. **[1]**
- Atrial systole raises atrial pressure and moves blood through open atrioventricular valves. **[1]**
- The signal is delayed at the atrioventricular node and conducted to the ventricles. **[1]**
- Ventricular systole raises ventricular pressure, closing atrioventricular valves. **[1]**
- Ventricular pressure above arterial pressure opens semilunar valves and blood is ejected. **[1]**
- During ventricular diastole, semilunar valves close and atrioventricular valves open when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure. **[1]**

_Examiner note: a memorized list of open and closed valves is a near miss. The valves change position because pressure on one side becomes greater than pressure on the other._

## Q6. Explain the pressure-flow mechanism for translocation in phloem. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- Sucrose is loaded into sieve tubes at a source, using active transport/ATP supplied by companion cells. **[1]**
- Loading sucrose lowers the water potential of the phloem. **[1]**
- Water enters from xylem by osmosis, producing high hydrostatic pressure at the source. **[1]**
- Sap moves by mass flow through sieve tubes from higher to lower hydrostatic pressure. **[1]**
- Sucrose is unloaded at a sink for use or storage, and water can leave the phloem. **[1]**
- Sieve plates permit flow between sieve tube elements / reduced cytoplasm and organelles lower resistance to flow. **[1]**

_Examiner note: "active transport moves sugar along the phloem" confuses loading with long-distance transport. ATP helps load sucrose; the sap then moves by pressure-driven mass flow._
