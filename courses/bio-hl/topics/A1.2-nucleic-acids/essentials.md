# A1.2 Nucleic acids — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Explain why DNA is the genetic material of living organisms without claiming that every virus uses DNA.
- [ ] Draw a nucleotide as a phosphate, a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base, with each part in the right place.
- [ ] Explain how condensation joins nucleotides into an RNA strand and creates a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
- [ ] Name the bases in DNA and RNA, and explain why their order carries information.
- [ ] Draw two antiparallel DNA strands with A–T and G–C pairs, showing hydrogen bonds between bases rather than along the backbone.
- [ ] Compare DNA and RNA by sugar, bases and usual number of strands, including the difference at the 2′ carbon of their sugars.
- [ ] Explain how complementary base pairing lets DNA be copied and information be expressed in RNA.
- [ ] Explain why the possible sequences of four bases give DNA a high capacity to store information.
- [ ] Explain how the near-universality of the genetic code supports common ancestry, without claiming that every organism has the same DNA sequence.

## Higher level

- [ ] Label the 5′ and 3′ ends of nucleic acid strands and explain why template strands run antiparallel to newly made strands.
- [ ] Explain why a purine pairing with a pyrimidine keeps the width of the DNA double helix consistent.
- [ ] Describe a nucleosome as DNA wound around an eight-histone core, with linker DNA and an associated linker histone.
- [ ] Explain how the Hershey–Chase radioisotope results identify DNA rather than protein as the genetic material of the bacteriophage.
- [ ] Interpret Chargaff’s base-composition data, including A≈T and G≈C within species and differing base proportions among species.

## Links to other topics

* **B1.2 Proteins** — a DNA base sequence ultimately specifies a protein's amino acid sequence. This connects a change in a gene to a possible change in protein shape and function.
* **D1.1 DNA replication** — complementary strands and 5′/3′ directionality explain how copies are made and why the two new strands are synthesized differently.
* **D1.2 Protein synthesis** — RNA uses complementary pairing to carry a copy of a gene's information; its base sequence is then read during translation.
* **D1.3 Mutation and gene editing** — a changed base alters the stored sequence. Keeping the base order separate from the sugar–phosphate backbone makes a mutation easier to describe accurately.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term                       | What it means                                                                                                    | What it’s not                                                 |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nucleotide                 | A phosphate group, a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base joined as one monomer                                  | A base on its own                                             |
| Sugar–phosphate backbone   | The covalently linked alternating sugars and phosphates of one strand                                            | The hydrogen bonds between DNA strands                        |
| Phosphodiester bond        | A covalent link joining the 3′ sugar position of one nucleotide through phosphate to the 5′ position of the next | A bond between complementary bases                            |
| Complementary base pairing | Specific pairing of A with T in DNA, or A with U in RNA, and G with C                                            | A claim that two whole DNA molecules have identical sequences |
| Antiparallel               | The two DNA strands run in opposite 5′ to 3′ directions                                                          | Two strands with opposite base identities at every position   |
| Genetic code               | The correspondence between mRNA codons and amino acids or stop signals                                           | The entire base sequence of one species' genome               |
| Purine                     | A two-ring base, adenine or guanine                                                                              | A one-ring pyrimidine, cytosine, thymine or uracil            |
| Nucleosome                 | DNA wrapped around a core of eight histone proteins                                                              | A nucleotide or a complete chromosome                         |
