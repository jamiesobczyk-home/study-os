# D3.3 Homeostasis — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Define homeostasis and explain why internal variables are kept within preset limits rather than at one unchanging value.
- [ ] Build a negative-feedback loop using a stimulus, receptor, control center, effector and response, and explain why positive feedback wouldn’t maintain stable conditions.
- [ ] Explain how insulin and glucagon regulate blood glucose concentration, including their sources, transport and effects on target cells.
- [ ] Compare the physiological causes, risk factors, prevention and treatment of type 1 and type 2 diabetes.
- [ ] Explain how peripheral thermoreceptors, the hypothalamus, pituitary gland and thyroxin contribute to thermoregulation.
- [ ] Explain how vasodilation and sweating increase heat loss when core body temperature rises.
- [ ] Explain how vasoconstriction, shivering, hair erection and uncoupled respiration in brown adipose tissue reduce heat loss or generate heat when core body temperature falls.
- [ ] Distinguish physiological thermoregulation from behavioral thermoregulation in birds and mammals.

## Higher level

- [ ] Distinguish excretion from osmoregulation and identify the functions of the main parts of a nephron.
- [ ] Explain ultrafiltration in the glomerulus and Bowman’s capsule, including why cells and large plasma proteins remain in the blood.
- [ ] Explain selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule, including active transport, cotransport, microvilli, mitochondria and osmosis.
- [ ] Explain how active transport of sodium ions from the ascending limb of the loop of Henle maintains a high osmotic concentration in the medulla.
- [ ] Explain how osmoreceptors, antidiuretic hormone and aquaporins change water reabsorption and urine concentration.
- [ ] Compare blood supply to skeletal muscles, the gut, brain and kidneys during sleep, wakeful rest and vigorous physical activity.

## Links to other topics

- **C2.1 Chemical signaling** — insulin, glucagon and antidiuretic hormone are signaling molecules. Receptors and target-cell responses explain why a hormone can circulate throughout the body but affect selected cells.
- **C1.2 Cell respiration** — shivering and brown adipose tissue release energy from respiratory substrates. Respiration explains where thermogenesis gets its energy.
- **B3.2 Transport** — hormones travel in blood, and changes in arteriole diameter redirect blood between organs. Knowledge of the circulation turns these into mechanisms rather than lists.
- **D2.3 Water potential** — water leaves collecting ducts by osmosis because the kidney medulla has a lower water potential. This connection explains how the kidney conserves water.
- **C3.1 Integration of body systems** — the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate effectors during exercise and temperature change. D3.3 provides named examples of that coordination.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homeostasis | Maintenance of an organism’s internal environment within preset limits despite internal or external changes | Keeping every variable perfectly constant |
| Set point | The target value around which a regulated variable fluctuates | A boundary that the variable must never cross |
| Negative feedback | Control in which a deviation triggers responses that reduce the deviation and return the variable toward its set point | A response that makes the original change larger |
| Positive feedback | Control in which a change triggers responses that amplify that change until a separate event stops the loop | The normal method for holding a homeostatic variable near its set point |
| Receptor | A cell or structure that detects a change in a regulated variable | The organ that carries out the corrective response |
| Effector | A muscle, gland or other tissue that produces a response | The structure that detects the original change |
| Endocrine cell | A hormone-secreting cell that releases its product into the blood | An exocrine cell that releases a product through a duct |
| Glycogenesis | Conversion of glucose into glycogen | Breakdown of glycogen to release glucose |
| Glycogenolysis | Breakdown of glycogen, producing glucose that the liver can release into the blood | Production of glycogen after a meal |
| Vasodilation | Widening of arterioles, increasing blood flow through a tissue | Widening of every blood vessel in the body |
| Vasoconstriction | Narrowing of arterioles, decreasing blood flow through a tissue | Complete closure of the tissue’s blood supply |
| Excretion | Removal from the body of waste products made by metabolism | Removal of undigested food in feces |
| Osmoregulation | Regulation of the osmotic concentration of body fluids | Removal of every dissolved substance from the blood |
| Ultrafiltration | Pressure-driven, non-selective movement of water and small solutes from glomerular blood into Bowman’s capsule | Selective recovery of useful substances from the filtrate |
| Selective reabsorption | Transfer of useful substances from nephron filtrate back into the blood | Filtration of plasma at the glomerulus |
| Osmotic concentration | Total concentration of dissolved particles, measured in osmoles per liter, osmol L−1 | The concentration of water alone |
| Aquaporin | A membrane channel that allows rapid movement of water | A pump that uses ATP to transport water |
| Antidiuretic hormone | A hormone that increases collecting-duct water permeability and reduces water loss in urine | A hormone that pumps water directly into the blood |
