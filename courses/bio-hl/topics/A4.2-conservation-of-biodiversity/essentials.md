# A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Define biodiversity and distinguish genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem diversity.
- [ ] Compare present biodiversity with past biodiversity while accounting for undiscovered species, an incomplete fossil record and differences between taxonomists.
- [ ] Explain how anthropogenic pressures can cause species extinction, using the North Island giant moa, Caribbean monk seal and passenger pigeon as case studies.
- [ ] Explain how human activity causes ecosystem loss, using Southeast Asian mixed dipterocarp forest and North American tallgrass prairie as case studies.
- [ ] Evaluate evidence for a biodiversity crisis from repeated surveys of species richness and evenness across many habitats.
- [ ] Explain how expert scientists, citizen scientists, peer review and published methods affect the reliability of biodiversity evidence.
- [ ] Explain how human population growth increases several direct pressures on biodiversity without treating population growth as the only cause.
- [ ] Link overexploitation, urbanization, deforestation, agricultural clearance, pollution and global transport to biodiversity loss.
- [ ] Compare in situ conservation, ex situ conservation, nature-reserve management, rewilding, ecosystem reclamation and germ-plasm storage.
- [ ] Explain why successful conservation usually combines several approaches instead of relying on one method.
- [ ] Distinguish rewilding from reclamation of a degraded ecosystem.
- [ ] Explain why conservation programs store seeds, cells or tissues as germ plasm and what this method can’t preserve by itself.
- [ ] Explain how evolutionary distinctness and global endangerment are combined to prioritize species in the EDGE of Existence programme.

## Links to other topics

- **A3.1 Diversity of organisms** — species definitions and genome variation affect how biodiversity is identified and measured. This connection helps you explain why species counts and genetic diversity aren’t perfectly known.
- **A4.1 Evolution and speciation** — speciation adds species, while extinction removes them. Evolutionary relationships also explain why losing an evolutionarily distinct species removes unusually unique history.
- **B4.2 Ecological niches** — habitat loss, invasive species and species removal alter interactions within communities. Knowing niches helps you predict why protecting one species without its habitat may fail.
- **C4.1 Populations and communities** — sampling, population change and community interactions supply evidence for biodiversity trends. This is where repeated surveys become more than lists of species.
- **D4.3 Climate change** — climate change can alter habitats and species distributions, adding pressure to the anthropogenic causes covered here.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biodiversity | The variety of life in all its forms, levels and combinations, including genetic, species and ecosystem diversity | Species richness alone |
| Genetic diversity | Variation in genetic information within a species or population, including variation in alleles | The number of species in a community |
| Species diversity | The variety of species in a community, including their richness and relative abundance | The number of individuals without regard to species |
| Ecosystem diversity | The variety of ecosystems, habitats and biological communities in an area | Variation between individuals of one species |
| Anthropogenic | Caused directly or indirectly by human activity | Any environmental change, including changes with entirely natural causes |
| Extinction | The loss of every living member of a species | A local population disappearing while the species survives elsewhere |
| Species richness | The number of different species present in a defined area | The relative abundance of those species |
| Species evenness | How similar the abundances of different species are in a community | The total number of species present |
| In situ conservation | Conservation of species in their natural habitats | Keeping organisms in zoos or seeds in banks |
| Ex situ conservation | Conservation outside a species’ natural habitat, such as in a zoo, botanic garden or bank | Protecting an intact wild habitat |
| Rewilding | Restoring natural ecological processes, often by reducing human control or reintroducing native species | Reconstructing a degraded site through intensive repair alone |
| Reclamation | Active restoration of a degraded ecosystem so that habitat and ecological functions recover | Preserving an ecosystem that hasn’t been degraded |
| Germ plasm | Living genetic material that can produce or help produce new organisms, such as seeds, cells or tissues | A complete ecosystem with its interactions intact |
| Evolutionary distinctness | The degree to which a species represents a long, relatively isolated branch of evolutionary history | Extinction risk by itself |
| EDGE species | A species that is both evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered | Any popular endangered species |
