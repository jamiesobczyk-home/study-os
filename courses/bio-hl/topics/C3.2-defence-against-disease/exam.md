# C3.2 Defence against disease — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Explain how a cut in the skin is sealed against blood loss and infection. **[5]**

A person receives a shallow cut that damages small blood vessels and breaks the skin barrier. Explain how a clot forms.

### Mark scheme

- Platelets adhere / aggregate at the damaged site and release clotting factors. **[1]**
- A cascade of reactions results in the formation of thrombin. **[1]**
- Thrombin catalyzes the conversion of soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin. **[1]**
- Fibrin forms a mesh that traps erythrocytes / stabilizes the platelet plug. **[1]**
- The clot reduces further blood loss and prevents entry of pathogens through the cut. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming fibrin earns little without the sequence. Full marks require thrombin acting on fibrinogen and the resulting fibrin mesh trapping cells._

## Q2. Distinguish the innate and adaptive immune systems. **[4]**

Give two paired differences between the innate and adaptive immune systems.

### Mark scheme

- The innate system responds to broad categories of pathogen, whereas the adaptive system responds to particular antigens / pathogens. **[1]**
- The innate response doesn’t become more effective through previous exposure, whereas the adaptive response retains memory cells. **[1]**
- The innate system includes phagocytes that engulf foreign material. **[1]**
- The adaptive system includes antigen-specific B-lymphocytes and helper T-lymphocytes / produces specific antibodies. **[1]**

_Examiner note: a list of cells without paired differences doesn’t fully answer “distinguish.” Each side needs a feature that separates it from the other._

## Q3. Explain the production of antibodies and immune memory after first exposure to an antigen. **[6]**

Begin with antigen-specific lymphocytes and end with the response to a second exposure.

### Mark scheme

- An antigen binds to / is recognized by a B-lymphocyte with a complementary specific receptor. **[1]**
- An activated helper T-lymphocyte recognizing the same antigen contacts the B-cell / releases cytokines that activate it. **[1]**
- The activated B-cell divides repeatedly by mitosis / undergoes clonal expansion. **[1]**
- Cells in the clone differentiate into plasma cells that secrete the specific antibody. **[1]**
- Some cells become long-lived memory cells. **[1]**
- On later exposure to the same antigen, memory cells produce a faster and larger secondary response. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “B-cells make antibodies” misses the selection, helper T-cell activation and cloning that the question asks for._

## Q4. Explain how HIV infection can lead to AIDS. **[4]**

Include the type of cell infected and the consequence for other infections.

### Mark scheme

- HIV infects helper T-lymphocytes / CD4 T-lymphocytes. **[1]**
- Infected helper T-cells are destroyed / their number decreases. **[1]**
- Reduced helper T-cell activity limits activation of B-cells / antibody production / adaptive immune responses. **[1]**
- Severe immune deficiency is AIDS and increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections. **[1]**

_Examiner note: HIV and AIDS can’t be used as interchangeable names. HIV is the virus; AIDS is the syndrome caused by severe loss of immune function._

## Q5. Explain the evolution of antibiotic resistance in a bacterial population. **[5]**

A patient takes an antibiotic that kills most, but not all, bacteria causing an infection. Explain how later bacterial generations can become resistant.

### Mark scheme

- Random mutation produces genetic variation in the bacterial population. **[1]**
- Some bacteria possess a heritable resistance allele before exposure to the antibiotic. **[1]**
- The antibiotic kills / inhibits susceptible bacteria, creating a selection pressure. **[1]**
- Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce by binary fission. **[1]**
- The resistance allele increases in frequency in later generations / a resistant strain develops. **[1]**

_Examiner note: the antibiotic doesn’t cause bacteria to mutate because they need protection. Full marks require pre-existing variation followed by differential survival and reproduction._

## Q6. Evaluate COVID-19 case data using percentage calculations. **[5]**

Region A recorded 200 new cases in week 1 and 150 in week 2. Region B recorded 120 new cases in week 1.

Calculate the percentage change in Region A from week 1 to week 2, calculate the percentage difference between the regions in week 1, and state one limitation of comparing the reported case numbers.

### Mark scheme

- Percentage change calculated as (150 − 200) ÷ 200 × 100. **[1]**
- Percentage change is −25% / a 25% decrease. **[1]**
- Percentage difference calculated as |200 − 120| ÷ 160 × 100, where 160 is the mean. **[1]**
- Percentage difference is 50%. **[1]**
- Accept: testing rates / reporting systems / population sizes / case definitions may differ between regions, limiting the comparison. **[1]**

_Examiner note: dividing both calculations by the first value loses the percentage-difference mark. Percentage change uses the old value; percentage difference uses the mean of the two values._
