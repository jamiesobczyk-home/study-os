# D4.2 Stability and change — where the marks go

## Defining stability as no change

**Commonly written:** “A stable ecosystem stays the same and its populations don’t change.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Stable ecosystems can fluctuate and experience disturbance. Stability concerns maintaining characteristic structure and processes or recovering after disturbance.

**Scores instead:** “A stable ecosystem maintains its characteristic structure and processes over time or recovers after disturbance, even though its populations fluctuate.”

## Treating the Amazon tipping point as a known percentage

**Commonly written:** “Once a fixed percentage of the Amazon is cut down, the forest will definitely collapse.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The threshold area is uncertain, and the required explanation is the feedback linking forest area, transpiration, rainfall and further tree loss.

**Scores instead:** “Deforestation can reduce transpiration and rainfall, causing drying, fire and further forest loss, although the minimum forest area needed to maintain rainfall is uncertain.”

## Calling every important predator a keystone species

**Commonly written:** “An apex predator is a keystone species because it’s at the top of the food chain.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Trophic position alone isn’t enough. A keystone species must have a disproportionately large effect on community structure relative to its abundance.

**Scores instead:** “A species is keystone when its removal causes a disproportionately large community change, such as sea-otter loss allowing urchins to remove kelp habitat.”

## Saying fertilizer directly removes oxygen

**Commonly written:** “Fertilizer enters the lake and uses up the oxygen, so fish die.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The missing mechanism is biological: nutrients increase producer growth, dead biomass increases decomposition, and aerobic microorganisms consume the dissolved oxygen.

**Scores instead:** “Nutrient enrichment causes a bloom, and decomposition of the resulting dead biomass raises biochemical oxygen demand and lowers dissolved oxygen.”

## Mixing up bioaccumulation and biomagnification

**Commonly written:** “Biomagnification is when a toxin builds up inside an organism during its lifetime.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Buildup within one organism is bioaccumulation. Biomagnification is the increase in concentration between successive trophic levels.

**Scores instead:** “A persistent pollutant bioaccumulates within organisms and biomagnifies as predators consume contaminated prey from lower trophic levels.”

## Assuming fragmentation removes plastic pollution

**Commonly written:** “Macroplastics eventually break down, so they stop being an environmental problem.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Fragmentation can turn larger debris into microplastics without biodegrading the polymer, so persistent particles remain available for ingestion.

**Scores instead:** “Macroplastics can fragment into microplastics, but the non-biodegradable material persists and can still harm or be eaten by marine organisms.”

## Treating rewilding as no human involvement

**Commonly written:** “Rewilding means people leave an ecosystem completely alone.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Rewilding can require deliberate restoration of species, habitat connections and natural processes while reducing continuing human control.

**Scores instead:** “Rewilding restores natural processes by actions such as reconnecting habitats, reintroducing keystone species and reducing repeated human disturbance.”

## Calling succession evolution

**Commonly written:** “Succession happens when pioneer species evolve into species that are better suited to the habitat.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Succession is replacement of populations in a community as conditions change, not genetic change turning one species into another.

**Scores instead:** “During succession, pioneer species alter conditions and are replaced by different species that can compete under the new conditions.”
