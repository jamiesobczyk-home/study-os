# D2.3 Water potential — where the marks go

## Describing osmosis as movement of solute

**Commonly written:** "Sucrose moves across the membrane from the concentrated solution to the dilute solution."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Osmosis is net movement of water through a partially permeable membrane, not movement of the dissolved solute.

**Scores instead:** "Water moves by osmosis through the partially permeable membrane from the hypotonic solution to the hypertonic solution."

## Treating tonicity as an absolute label

**Commonly written:** "This solution is hypertonic."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Hypertonic and hypotonic compare two solutions, so the answer must state which other solution has the lower solute concentration.

**Scores instead:** "The surrounding solution is hypertonic relative to the cell because it has the higher solute concentration."

## Saying water stops moving at equilibrium

**Commonly written:** "In an isotonic solution, water no longer crosses the membrane."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Water continues moving both ways at equal rates; only net movement and overall volume change are zero.

**Scores instead:** "At isotonic dynamic equilibrium, water crosses in both directions at equal rates, so there is no net movement."

## Saying the cell wall prevents osmosis

**Commonly written:** "A plant cell doesn’t take in water because its cell wall blocks it."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Water crosses the cell membrane; the wall resists expansion and produces rising turgor pressure rather than blocking entry.

**Scores instead:** "Water enters by osmosis until increasing turgor pressure raises the cell's water potential to equilibrium."

## Calling an animal cell plasmolyzed

**Commonly written:** "An animal cell in a hypertonic solution becomes plasmolyzed."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Plasmolysis is separation of a membrane from a cell wall, while a water-losing animal cell shrinks and becomes crenated.

**Scores instead:** "The animal cell loses water by osmosis, shrinks and becomes crenated."

## Comparing solute potentials instead of water potentials

**Commonly written:** "Water moves into cell X because its solute potential is more negative."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Pressure potential also contributes to total water potential, which must be calculated before predicting net water movement.

**Scores instead:** "Calculate Ψw = Ψs + Ψp for both cells; water then moves from the higher Ψw to the lower Ψw."

## Thinking every pressure potential is positive

**Commonly written:** "Pressure potential inside plant transport tissue is always positive."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Pressure is generally positive inside walled cells, but xylem sap under transpiration tension can have negative pressure potential.

**Scores instead:** "Pressure potential is generally positive in living cells but can be negative in xylem carrying sap under tension."
