# B2.2 Organelles and compartmentalization — where the marks go

## Defining every organelle as membrane-bound

**Commonly written:** "An organelle is a membrane-bound structure with a specific function."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The required definition is a discrete cell subunit adapted for a function. Ribosomes count as organelles despite lacking membranes.

**Scores instead:** "An organelle is a discrete subunit of a cell adapted to perform a specific function, so ribosomes are included."

## Saying compartmentalization only keeps the cell organized

**Commonly written:** "Compartments make the cell more organized and efficient."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The answer gives no mechanism. The mark requires concentration of enzymes and metabolites or separation of incompatible processes.

**Scores instead:** "A compartment concentrates enzymes and substrates in a small volume, increasing the frequency of enzyme-substrate collisions."

## Giving the nucleus only a protective function

**Commonly written:** "The nuclear envelope protects the DNA from the cytoplasm."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The required advantage is separation of transcription from translation, which allows mRNA processing before contact with ribosomes.

**Scores instead:** "The nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation, allowing mRNA to be processed before it leaves through nuclear pores."

## Confusing the mitochondrial matrix with the intermembrane space

**Commonly written:** "The Krebs cycle occurs in the intermembrane space, where its enzymes are concentrated."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates are in the matrix. The intermembrane space is the small compartment where protons accumulate.

**Scores instead:** "The Krebs cycle occurs in the matrix, while protons accumulate in the small intermembrane space."

## Saying cristae increase mitochondrial volume

**Commonly written:** "Cristae increase the mitochondrion's volume so it can contain more enzymes."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Cristae increase inner-membrane surface area, providing more space for electron transport chains and ATP synthase.

**Scores instead:** "Cristae increase the inner membrane's surface area for electron transport chains and ATP synthase."

## Switching the stroma and thylakoid lumen

**Commonly written:** "The Calvin cycle occurs in the thylakoid lumen, while protons collect in the stroma."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The locations are reversed. Calvin cycle enzymes are in the stroma, while protons accumulate inside thylakoids.

**Scores instead:** "The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma, and protons accumulate in the small thylakoid lumen."

## Sending secreted proteins from free ribosomes to the Golgi

**Commonly written:** "A free ribosome makes a secreted protein, which then moves directly to the Golgi."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Secreted proteins are synthesized on rough-ER-bound ribosomes and travel from the ER to the Golgi in vesicles.

**Scores instead:** "A rough-ER-bound ribosome synthesizes the protein, which enters the ER and travels to the Golgi in a transport vesicle."

## Making clathrin the vesicle's permanent membrane

**Commonly written:** "Clathrin forms the vesicle membrane and stays around it until the cargo is released."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The vesicle membrane is a phospholipid bilayer. Clathrin is a temporary protein coat that helps curve the membrane during budding.

**Scores instead:** "Clathrin forms a temporary coat that helps curve a donor membrane into a budding vesicle."
