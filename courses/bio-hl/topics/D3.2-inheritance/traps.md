# D3.2 Inheritance — where the marks go

## Calling a dominant allele common or strong

**Commonly written:** “The dominant allele is stronger and occurs more often in the population.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Dominance describes the phenotype of a heterozygote. It says nothing about an allele’s frequency, benefit or physical strength.

**Scores instead:** “A dominant allele affects the phenotype when one copy is present in a heterozygote.”

## Treating phenotype as genotype alone

**Commonly written:** “An organism’s phenotype is determined by its genotype.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Phenotype can result from genotype, environmental factors and their interaction, including environmentally altered gene expression.

**Scores instead:** “Phenotype results from genotype and environmental factors, which may interact by changing patterns of gene expression.”

## Confusing multiple alleles with polygenic inheritance

**Commonly written:** “Skin color is controlled by multiple alleles, so it shows continuous variation.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Multiple alleles are alternative forms of one gene. Skin color is polygenic because several genes contribute to the characteristic.

**Scores instead:** “Human skin color shows continuous variation because multiple genes and environmental factors contribute to the phenotype.”

## Giving a haemophilia allele to the Y chromosome

**Commonly written:** “An affected male has the genotype X^hY^h.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The notation assigns the haemophilia allele to the X chromosome. The Y chromosome doesn’t carry the corresponding allele in this cross.

**Scores instead:** “An affected male has the genotype X^hY because his single X chromosome carries the recessive haemophilia allele.”

## Assuming four dihybrid gametes are always equally frequent

**Commonly written:** “Every AaBb individual makes AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes in equal numbers.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Equal frequencies require independent assortment. Linked genes produce more parental gametes than recombinant gametes.

**Scores instead:** “An AaBb individual produces four gamete types equally only when the genes assort independently.”

## Calling every offspring from a test cross recombinant

**Commonly written:** “Crossing a heterozygote with a double-recessive individual produces recombinant offspring.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Recombinants have allele combinations different from the parental chromosome combinations; the remaining offspring belong to parental classes.

**Scores instead:** “In an AB/ab test cross, AB and ab offspring are parental classes, while Ab and aB offspring are recombinant classes.”

## Saying a non-significant chi-squared result proves the null hypothesis

**Commonly written:** “The chi-squared value is below the critical value, so the null hypothesis is proved.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A non-significant result shows insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. It can’t prove that the hypothesis is true.

**Scores instead:** “Because p is greater than 0.05, the difference isn’t statistically significant and the null hypothesis isn’t rejected.”
