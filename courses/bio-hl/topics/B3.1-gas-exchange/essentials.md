# B3.1 Gas exchange — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Explain why gas exchange becomes harder as an organism gets larger, using surface area-to-volume ratio and diffusion distance.
- [ ] Explain how permeability, moisture, a thin barrier and a large surface area increase diffusion across a gas-exchange surface.
- [ ] Explain how ventilation, dense capillary networks and continuous blood flow maintain concentration gradients in lungs and gills.
- [ ] Relate alveoli, branching bronchioles, extensive capillary beds and pulmonary surfactant to efficient gas exchange in mammalian lungs.
- [ ] Explain inhalation and exhalation using the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, abdominal muscles, thoracic volume and pressure.
- [ ] Distinguish tidal volume, vital capacity, inspiratory reserve and expiratory reserve, and identify them on lung-volume data.
- [ ] Explain how the cuticle, epidermis, stomata, guard cells, mesophyll air spaces and veins contribute to gas exchange in a leaf.
- [ ] Draw and label a plan diagram showing the distribution of tissues in a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf.
- [ ] Explain why opening stomata for gas exchange leads to transpiration, and predict how light, temperature, humidity and wind affect its rate.
- [ ] Calculate stomatal density from microscope observations and explain why repeated counts improve reliability.

## Higher level

- [ ] Explain reversible and cooperative binding of oxygen to haemoglobin, and explain why foetal haemoglobin has a higher oxygen affinity than adult haemoglobin.
- [ ] Explain how carbon dioxide lowers haemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen through the Bohr shift, including its allosteric effect.
- [ ] Interpret oxygen dissociation curves, including sigmoid shape, loading, unloading and left or right shifts.

## Links to other topics

- **B2.1 Membranes and membrane transport** — gas exchange depends on diffusion through cell-surface membranes. This link lets you explain movement instead of saying that gases “enter” or “leave.”
- **B3.2 Transport** — blood carries gases between lungs and tissues, while xylem replaces water lost through stomata. Gas exchange explains why both transport systems are needed.
- **C1.2 Cell respiration** — aerobic respiration consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. This supplies the concentration changes behind gas exchange at active tissues.
- **C1.3 Photosynthesis** — photosynthesis consumes carbon dioxide and produces oxygen. Leaf structure connects those reactions inside chloroplasts to gases in the atmosphere.
- **D2.3 Water potential** — evaporation from mesophyll cell walls and water movement through leaves depend on water-potential gradients. This explains the water cost of opening stomata.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gas exchange | Diffusion of gases between an organism and its environment | Ventilation alone; moving air doesn’t guarantee diffusion across a surface |
| Ventilation | Movement of air or water over a gas-exchange surface | The chemical release of energy in respiration |
| Gas-exchange surface | A permeable surface across which respiratory or photosynthetic gases diffuse | The entire lung, gill or leaf regardless of which tissues gases cross |
| Concentration gradient | A difference in concentration that produces net diffusion from higher to lower concentration | A difference created by gases being actively pumped across the surface |
| Alveolus | A microscopic air sac in a mammalian lung where gases diffuse between air and blood | A bronchiole, which conducts air toward alveoli |
| Surfactant | A secretion that reduces surface tension in the moist lining of alveoli and helps prevent collapse | Mucus blocking or trapping gases at the exchange surface |
| Inhalation | Ventilation into the lungs caused by increased thoracic volume and decreased pressure | Oxygen diffusing from an alveolus into blood |
| Tidal volume | The volume inhaled or exhaled during one normal resting breath | The greatest volume a person can exhale |
| Vital capacity | The greatest volume exhaled after the deepest possible inhalation | Total lung capacity; vital capacity excludes residual volume |
| Stoma | A pore in the epidermis through which gases and water vapor move | A guard cell; two guard cells control the pore |
| Transpiration | Loss of water vapor from a plant, mainly through stomata | Water uptake by roots or the movement of liquid water through xylem |
| Stomatal density | Number of stomata per unit leaf area | Percentage of stomata that are open |
| Oxygen affinity | The tendency of haemoglobin to bind and retain oxygen | The amount of haemoglobin in a sample of blood |
| Cooperative binding | Binding in which one oxygen molecule increases haemoglobin’s affinity for the next oxygen molecule | Four oxygen molecules attaching independently with unchanged affinity |
| Bohr shift | Reduced oxygen affinity of haemoglobin when carbon dioxide rises and pH falls | Movement of haemoglobin or red blood cells toward active tissue |
| Oxygen dissociation curve | A graph of haemoglobin oxygen saturation against oxygen partial pressure | A graph showing the rate of ventilation against carbon dioxide concentration |
