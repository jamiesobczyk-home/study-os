# A4.1 Evolution and speciation — where the marks go

## Calling an acquired change evolution

**Commonly written:** "A giraffe stretches its neck during life, so the longer neck is an evolutionary change."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Evolution requires a change in heritable characteristics of a population across generations, not an acquired change in one organism.

**Scores instead:** "Evolution occurs when the frequency of heritable characteristics in a population changes over generations."

## Treating sequence similarity as proof of the same species

**Commonly written:** "Their DNA sequences are similar, so the organisms are the same species."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Sequence similarity supports common ancestry and can indicate relatedness, but distinct species can still share highly similar sequences.

**Scores instead:** "Greater sequence similarity supports common ancestry and generally indicates that the species diverged more recently."

## Defining homologous structures by function

**Commonly written:** "Bird wings and insect wings are homologous because both are used for flying."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Shared function identifies analogy here; homology requires a shared evolutionary origin and underlying structural plan.

**Scores instead:** "Bird and insect wings are analogous flight structures because they have similar functions but different evolutionary origins."

## Calling every evolutionary change speciation

**Commonly written:** "When a population changes over time, a new species has formed."

**Why it doesn’t score:** A population can evolve while remaining one species; speciation requires splitting, divergence and reproductive isolation.

**Scores instead:** "Speciation occurs when populations diverge and become reproductively isolated, producing separate species."

## Saying isolation directly creates adaptations

**Commonly written:** "The Congo River caused bonobos and chimpanzees to develop different adaptations."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The river restricted gene flow; different selection pressures acting on heritable variation caused the populations to diverge.

**Scores instead:** "The Congo River reduced gene flow, allowing differential selection to produce divergence and eventual reproductive isolation."

## Confusing sympatric speciation with unrestricted interbreeding

**Commonly written:** "Sympatric speciation happens in the same place, so the populations keep exchanging alleles."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Sympatric populations share an area but must have behavioral, temporal or genetic barriers that reduce gene flow.

**Scores instead:** "Sympatric speciation occurs in the same geographical area when reproductive barriers prevent gene flow between populations."

## Saying a mule is a new fertile species

**Commonly written:** "A horse and donkey produce a mule, so hybridization has created a new species."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Mules are normally sterile because their chromosomes can’t pair normally during meiosis, so they don’t form a reproducing population.

**Scores instead:** "A mule is a normally sterile interspecific hybrid, and its sterility prevents continued gene flow between horses and donkeys."

## Saying chromosome doubling always causes sterility

**Commonly written:** "Chromosome doubling makes a plant sterile because it has too many chromosomes."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Doubling can provide homologous partners and restore balanced meiosis, while chromosome incompatibility isolates the polyploid from its parents.

**Scores instead:** "Chromosome doubling can restore fertility in a hybrid and create reproductive isolation from both parent species."
