# C3.2 Defence against disease — where the marks go

## Making fibrinogen form the mesh

**Commonly written:** “Fibrinogen forms a mesh over the wound and traps red blood cells.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Fibrinogen is the soluble precursor. Thrombin converts it into insoluble fibrin, which forms the mesh.

**Scores instead:** “Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, which forms a mesh and traps erythrocytes.”

## Calling every defense adaptive

**Commonly written:** “Phagocytes are part of adaptive immunity because they respond when a pathogen enters.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Phagocytes respond broadly to foreign material and don’t produce antigen-specific immune memory, so they belong to innate immunity.

**Scores instead:** “Phagocytes are part of innate immunity because they engulf broad categories of foreign material without antigen-specific memory.”

## Swapping antigens and antibodies

**Commonly written:** “The pathogen produces antibodies that the B-cell recognizes.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Pathogens carry antigens. Activated B-cell clones produce antibodies that bind specifically to those antigens.

**Scores instead:** “A pathogen carries antigens, and activated plasma cells secrete antibodies that bind to the corresponding antigen.”

## Activating a B-cell with antigen alone

**Commonly written:** “When a B-cell meets its antigen, it immediately starts producing antibodies.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The required model includes contact with the specific antigen and activation signals from a helper T-cell recognizing the same antigen.

**Scores instead:** “A matching B-cell is activated after encountering its antigen and receiving cytokine signals from an activated helper T-cell.”

## Treating HIV and AIDS as the same thing

**Commonly written:** “AIDS infects helper T-cells and spreads in body fluids.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** HIV is the transmissible virus that infects helper T-cells. AIDS is the severe immune-deficiency syndrome that can follow.

**Scores instead:** “HIV infects and destroys helper T-cells, and a severe resulting loss of immune function is called AIDS.”

## Giving antibiotics to attack viruses

**Commonly written:** “Antibiotics kill viruses by stopping their cell-wall synthesis.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Viruses have no cell wall, ribosomes or independent metabolism, so antibiotics aimed at bacterial processes have no viral target.

**Scores instead:** “Antibiotics can inhibit bacterial processes such as cell-wall synthesis, but viruses lack those cellular targets.”

## Saying antibiotics create resistance mutations

**Commonly written:** “Bacteria mutate to become resistant when they’re exposed to an antibiotic.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Mutations occur randomly before selection. The antibiotic favors bacteria that already carry heritable resistance.

**Scores instead:** “The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria, while pre-existing resistant variants survive, reproduce and pass on resistance.”

## Giving herd immunity to an individual

**Commonly written:** “An unvaccinated person becomes immune when enough other people are vaccinated.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Herd immunity reduces the chance of exposure by interrupting transmission; it doesn’t create memory cells in that individual.

**Scores instead:** “A high immune proportion interrupts transmission and indirectly lowers the infection risk for people who aren’t immune.”
