# D4.2 Stability and change — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Explain what ecosystem stability means and use the long-term continuity of forests, deserts or another ecosystem as evidence, without claiming that every population stays constant.
- [ ] Explain why a stable ecosystem needs an energy supply, nutrient recycling, genetic diversity and climatic variables that remain within organisms’ tolerance ranges.
- [ ] Explain how Amazon deforestation could cross a tipping point by reducing transpiration, cooling, air flow and rainfall, and calculate percentage forest loss from area data.
- [ ] Design a mesocosm investigation of ecosystem stability, including the independent variable, measurements, controls and the difference between transfers of matter and energy.
- [ ] Explain why removing a keystone species can change community structure or cause ecosystem collapse even when that species isn't abundant.
- [ ] Assess whether harvesting a named terrestrial plant and a named marine fish is sustainable by comparing harvest and replacement rates and using population data.
- [ ] Assess agricultural sustainability using soil erosion, nutrient leaching, fertilizers and other inputs, agrochemical pollution and carbon footprint.
- [ ] Explain the full sequence from fertilizer leaching to eutrophication, increased biochemical oxygen demand and the death of aquatic organisms.
- [ ] Distinguish bioaccumulation from biomagnification and explain why DDT or mercury reaches high concentrations in consumers at high trophic levels.
- [ ] Compare the effects of macroplastic and microplastic pollution on marine organisms and explain why both persist.
- [ ] Explain how rewilding restores natural processes through keystone-species reintroduction, habitat connectivity and reduced human impact, using Hinewai Reserve as an example.

## Higher level

- [ ] Define ecological succession and explain how changes in abiotic conditions or biotic factors can cause one community to replace another.
- [ ] Describe primary succession from a bare surface with no soil, including changes in plant size, primary production, species diversity, food-web complexity and nutrient cycling.
- [ ] Explain cyclical succession using a named example in which communities recur instead of reaching one permanently unchanged community.
- [ ] Distinguish a climax community from arrested succession and explain how grazing livestock or wetland drainage can prevent the expected climax community from developing.

## Links to other topics

- **C4.2 Transfers of energy and matter** — D4.2 depends on energy flowing through ecosystems while nutrients are recycled. That distinction explains both stability and why sealed mesocosms still need light.
- **B4.2 Ecological niches** — keystone species, succession and food-web change make more sense when each species’ niche is treated as a set of interactions rather than a location.
- **A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity** — genetic and species diversity can increase resilience, while rewilding and habitat connectivity apply conservation methods to ecosystem processes.
- **D4.1 Natural selection** — genetic diversity gives populations variation on which natural selection can act when conditions change.
- **D4.3 Climate change** — climatic variables can leave organisms’ tolerance ranges, and feedback involving forests, rainfall and fire can push ecosystems toward a different state.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ecosystem stability | The ability of an ecosystem to maintain its structure and processes over time or recover after disturbance | Every population remaining at one fixed size |
| Tolerance range | The range of an abiotic variable within which an organism can survive and function | The single condition at which an organism functions best |
| Tipping point | A threshold beyond which a small additional change can drive a large, potentially self-reinforcing ecosystem change | Any gradual or reversible change in an ecosystem |
| Mesocosm | A bounded, simplified ecosystem used to investigate ecological variables under partly controlled conditions | A completely isolated system with no energy exchange |
| Keystone species | A species with an effect on community structure that is disproportionately large relative to its abundance | Any abundant species or every apex predator |
| Sustainability | Use of a resource at a rate that allows its continued replacement and future availability | Avoiding all harvesting or environmental change |
| Eutrophication | Nutrient enrichment of water that can cause excessive producer growth, decomposition and oxygen depletion | Direct poisoning of aquatic animals by fertilizer |
| Biochemical oxygen demand | The oxygen required by microorganisms to decompose organic matter in water under specified conditions | The amount of oxygen produced by algae |
| Bioaccumulation | Increase in a pollutant’s concentration within one organism over time because uptake exceeds elimination | Increase in concentration between trophic levels |
| Biomagnification | Increase in the concentration of a persistent pollutant in consumers at successively higher trophic levels | A pollutant becoming more concentrated within one individual over time |
| Macroplastic | Plastic debris generally larger than 5 mm | Plastic that has biodegraded into harmless substances |
| Microplastic | Plastic particles generally smaller than 5 mm | Any plastic object that can be seen without magnification |
| Rewilding | Restoration of natural ecosystem processes with reduced continuing human control | Returning an ecosystem to one exact historical species list |
| Ecological succession | Change in community composition over time, often as organisms alter conditions and are replaced by others | Evolution within one population |
| Pioneer species | Early colonizers that can survive the conditions at the start of succession | The final species to enter a climax community |
| Climax community | A relatively stable community toward which succession tends under particular environmental conditions | A community in which no organisms die or populations change |
| Arrested succession | Succession prevented from reaching its expected climax community by repeated disturbance or human influence | Primary succession that has permanently ended |
