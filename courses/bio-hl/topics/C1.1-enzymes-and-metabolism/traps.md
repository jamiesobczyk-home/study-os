# C1.1 Enzymes and metabolism — where the marks go

## Saying enzymes give reactions energy

**Commonly written:** "Enzymes provide the energy needed for reactions."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Enzymes lower the activation-energy barrier. They don’t supply energy or change the energy difference between reactants and products.

**Scores instead:** "An enzyme provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, increasing the proportion of collisions that lead to reaction."

## Describing the active site as a rigid lock

**Commonly written:** "The substrate fits perfectly into an active site that keeps the same shape."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The syllabus requires induced fit, in which binding causes conformational changes in both the enzyme and substrate.

**Scores instead:** "Substrate binding changes the conformations of the enzyme and substrate, producing an induced fit that favors catalysis."

## Saying low temperature denatures enzymes

**Commonly written:** "Cold conditions denature the enzyme, so its activity falls."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Low temperature usually reduces kinetic energy and successful collision frequency without permanently changing the enzyme’s conformation.

**Scores instead:** "At low temperature, reduced molecular motion causes fewer successful enzyme–substrate collisions, so the reaction rate falls."

## Calling a substrate-concentration plateau denaturation

**Commonly written:** "Too much substrate denatures the enzyme, causing the graph to level off."

**Why it doesn’t score:** The plateau occurs because active sites are saturated and enzyme concentration limits the rate, not because substrate damages the enzyme.

**Scores instead:** "At high substrate concentration, active sites are occupied as often as possible, so adding more substrate doesn’t increase the rate."

## Saying every inhibitor binds to the active site

**Commonly written:** "Competitive and non-competitive inhibitors both block the active site by occupying it."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Competitive inhibitors occupy the active site, while non-competitive inhibitors bind to a separate allosteric site and alter active-site conformation.

**Scores instead:** "A competitive inhibitor binds to the active site, whereas a non-competitive inhibitor binds allosterically and changes the active site’s shape."

## Saying extra substrate overcomes any reversible inhibition

**Commonly written:** "Adding enough substrate restores the original rate with either reversible inhibitor."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Extra substrate can outcompete a competitive inhibitor, but it can’t restore enzymes made non-functional by allosteric non-competitive binding.

**Scores instead:** "Increasing substrate concentration can overcome competitive inhibition but can’t restore the uninhibited maximum rate during non-competitive inhibition."

## Treating feedback inhibition as permanent enzyme damage

**Commonly written:** "Isoleucine permanently destroys threonine deaminase when enough has been produced."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Feedback inhibition is reversible regulation. Isoleucine binds allosterically, and inhibition is relieved when its concentration falls.

**Scores instead:** "Accumulated isoleucine reversibly inhibits threonine deaminase allosterically, slowing the first step until isoleucine concentration decreases."

## Saying penicillin directly dissolves the bacterial wall

**Commonly written:** "Penicillin breaks apart the bacterial cell wall."

**Why it doesn’t score:** Penicillin irreversibly inhibits transpeptidase, preventing new peptidoglycan cross-links and leaving the wall vulnerable during growth.

**Scores instead:** "Penicillin covalently inactivates transpeptidase, reducing peptidoglycan cross-linking so the weakened bacterial cell wall can fail."
