## Core

There’s no SL content in A3.2. Every syllabus statement in this topic is higher level.

## Higher level

- [ ] Explain why biologists classify organisms and how classification makes further study possible.
- [ ] List the traditional taxonomic ranks in order and explain why fixed ranks don’t always match evolutionary divergence.
- [ ] Explain the advantages of classifying organisms by evolutionary relationships, including the prediction of shared characteristics.
- [ ] Define a clade as a common ancestor and all of its descendants, then identify complete clades on a cladogram.
- [ ] Compare morphological and molecular evidence for placing organisms in clades, including how convergent evolution can mislead classification.
- [ ] Explain how DNA base sequences or protein amino acid sequences are compared to infer evolutionary relationships.
- [ ] Explain how parsimony analysis selects the cladogram requiring the smallest number of sequence changes.
- [ ] Interpret the root, nodes and terminal branches of a cladogram.
- [ ] Use the position of nodes to determine which organisms share the most recent common ancestor.
- [ ] Explain why rotating branches around a node doesn’t change the evolutionary relationships shown.
- [ ] Explain how the gradual accumulation of sequence differences provides a molecular clock for estimating divergence time.
- [ ] Explain why molecular-clock dates are estimates, including effects of generation time, population size and selection pressure.
- [ ] Explain how molecular evidence can falsify a traditional classification based on misleading morphological similarities.
- [ ] Outline how comparisons of rRNA base sequences led to the three-domain classification of Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.

## Links to other topics

- **A1.2 Nucleic acids** — cladistics uses differences in DNA and RNA base sequences as evidence. Knowing what a base sequence represents makes molecular comparisons easier to explain.
- **A3.1 Diversity of organisms** — species names and traditional taxonomic ranks organize diversity, while A3.2 asks whether those groups reflect common ancestry.
- **A4.1 Evolution and speciation** — cladograms represent hypotheses about descent after lineages diverge. Homologous and analogous structures explain why morphology can support or mislead a classification.
- **D1.2 Protein synthesis** — DNA sequence differences can produce amino acid sequence differences. That connection explains why either genes or proteins can supply evidence for a cladogram.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classification | Placing organisms into groups using shared characteristics or evolutionary relationships | Naming an organism without grouping it |
| Taxon | Any named group in a classification system, such as a family, genus or species | A taxonomic rank itself; “family” is a rank, while Felidae is a taxon |
| Traditional hierarchy | The ranked sequence kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species | A cladogram or an unranked system of clades |
| Clade | A common ancestor together with all of its descendants | Any group of similar organisms or a group missing some descendants |
| Cladistics | Classification based on hypotheses of common ancestry | Classification based only on visible resemblance |
| Cladogram | A branching diagram showing a hypothesis about evolutionary relationships | A ladder of progress or automatically a timeline |
| Root | The ancestral starting point shared by all organisms represented in a rooted cladogram | The least evolved living organism |
| Node | A branch point representing a hypothetical common ancestor where lineages diverged | A living species positioned between two other species |
| Terminal branch | A branch leading to a taxon being compared | Proof that the taxon has stopped evolving |
| Most recent common ancestor | The latest ancestral population shared by the organisms being compared | The modern organism drawn nearest to them |
| Molecular clock | An estimate of divergence time based on the gradual accumulation of sequence differences | A clock with one exact mutation rate for every gene and lineage |
| Parsimony analysis | Selection of the cladogram that explains the data with the smallest number of sequence changes | Proof that the selected cladogram is certainly the true history |
| Convergent evolution | Independent evolution of similar features in different lineages under similar selection pressures | Inheritance of a feature from a recent common ancestor |
| Analogous structures | Features with similar functions but different evolutionary origins | Homologous structures inherited from a common ancestor |
| rRNA | Ribosomal RNA, whose base sequences can be compared across organisms as evidence of ancestry | The DNA sequence of one rapidly changing gene |
| Domain | A taxonomic level above kingdom; the three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya | Another name for a kingdom |
