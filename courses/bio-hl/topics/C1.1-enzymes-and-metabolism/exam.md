# C1.1 Enzymes and metabolism — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Explain how an enzyme catalyzes a reaction according to the induced-fit model. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Substrate collides with / binds to a compatible active site. **[1]**
- Binding causes conformational change in the enzyme and substrate / induced fit. **[1]**
- Catalytic groups are correctly positioned / substrate bonds are strained or weakened, lowering activation energy. **[1]**
- Products are released and the enzyme remains available for reuse. **[1]**

_Examiner note: a lock-and-key description scores at most for binding. Full marks require conformational change and an explanation of how induced fit favors catalysis._

## Q2. Explain the effects of temperature and substrate concentration on the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- Increasing temperature raises kinetic energy / molecular motion and collision frequency. **[1]**
- The rate increases to an optimum because successful collisions become more frequent. **[1]**
- Above the optimum, interactions maintaining enzyme conformation are disrupted / the active site changes as the enzyme denatures. **[1]**
- Increasing substrate concentration initially increases collisions with available active sites. **[1]**
- At high substrate concentration, active sites are occupied as often as possible / become saturated. **[1]**
- The rate plateaus because enzyme concentration becomes limiting. **[1]**

_Examiner note: temperature and substrate concentration produce plateaus or declines for different reasons. Saturation doesn’t denature the enzyme, while excessive temperature can._

## Q3. Distinguish anabolic and catabolic reactions, giving two examples of each. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Anabolic reactions synthesize larger / more complex molecules from smaller substances and require an energy input. **[1]**
- Accept: protein synthesis / glycogen formation / photosynthesis as anabolic examples. **[1]**
- Catabolic reactions break down or oxidize molecules and release usable energy. **[1]**
- Accept: hydrolysis in digestion / oxidation of substrates in cell respiration as catabolic examples. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming examples without explaining the direction of molecular change doesn’t distinguish the two types of metabolism._

## Q4. Compare and contrast competitive and non-competitive inhibition. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- Both reduce the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction / both involve specific binding to an enzyme. **[1]**
- A competitive inhibitor binds reversibly to the active site / competes with the substrate. **[1]**
- A non-competitive inhibitor binds reversibly to a specific allosteric site. **[1]**
- Allosteric binding causes conformational change that alters the active site / prevents catalysis. **[1]**
- Increasing substrate concentration can overcome competitive inhibition / restore the uninhibited maximum rate. **[1]**
- Increasing substrate concentration cannot restore the uninhibited maximum rate during non-competitive inhibition. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “non-competitive inhibitors don’t compete” is incomplete. The answer needs the allosteric binding site, conformational change and different response to extra substrate._

## Q5. Explain how feedback inhibition regulates the pathway that converts threonine to isoleucine. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Isoleucine is the end product of the metabolic pathway. **[1]**
- Accumulated isoleucine binds to an allosteric site on threonine deaminase / the enzyme catalyzing the first step. **[1]**
- Binding changes the active site’s conformation and reduces enzyme activity / pathway rate. **[1]**
- When isoleucine concentration falls, inhibition is relieved, preventing unnecessary use of substrates and energy. **[1]**

_Examiner note: saying that isoleucine “stops its production” misses the mechanism. Full marks require the first enzyme, its allosteric site and reversible regulation._

## Q6. Explain how penicillin acts as a mechanism-based inhibitor and suggest how a changed transpeptidase can confer resistance. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Penicillin binds at the active site of bacterial transpeptidase. **[1]**
- Penicillin forms an irreversible covalent attachment / chemically changes and inactivates the active site. **[1]**
- Transpeptidase can no longer catalyze cross-linking of peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall. **[1]**
- The weakened cell wall makes growing bacteria susceptible to osmotic lysis. **[1]**
- Accept: mutation alters transpeptidase so penicillin binds less effectively while the enzyme can still catalyze peptidoglycan cross-linking. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “penicillin destroys the cell wall” skips the scored chain. The inhibitor acts on transpeptidase, cross-linking decreases, and the weakened wall leaves the cell vulnerable to lysis._
