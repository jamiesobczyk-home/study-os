# D2.2 Gene expression — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

There’s no SL content in D2.2. Every syllabus point in this topic is higher level.

## Higher level

- [ ] Explain how transcription, translation and the function of a protein product connect a gene to a cell’s phenotype.
- [ ] Explain how promoters, enhancers and transcription factors regulate whether and how rapidly a gene is transcribed.
- [ ] Explain how the rate of mRNA degradation affects translation and protein production.
- [ ] Explain how selective gene expression produces stable patterns of cell differentiation during epigenesis without changing DNA base sequences.
- [ ] Distinguish the genome, transcriptome and proteome of a cell, including which can change with cell type and time.
- [ ] Explain how methylation of promoter DNA and methylation of histones can affect transcription.
- [ ] Explain how epigenetic tags can pass patterns of gene expression to daughter cells or offspring without changing DNA nucleotide sequences.
- [ ] Outline how air pollution can alter DNA methylation and therefore gene expression.
- [ ] Explain why the removal of most, but not all, epigenetic tags from parental genomes allows both developmental resetting and genomic imprinting.
- [ ] Use ligers and tigons to illustrate how parent-of-origin epigenetic tags can affect phenotype.
- [ ] Explain how monozygotic twin studies can investigate environmental effects on gene expression, including what the studies can’t prove.
- [ ] Explain how lactose regulates transcription of the lac operon in *E. coli*.
- [ ] Explain how a steroid hormone such as estrogen can alter gene expression through an intracellular receptor.
- [ ] Construct a complete explanation connecting an external signal, gene regulation, protein production and a changed phenotype.

## Links to other topics

- **D1.2 Protein synthesis** — transcription and translation are the middle of the gene-to-phenotype chain. D1.2 supplies the mechanism, while D2.2 explains how the cell controls it.
- **B2.3 Cell specialization** — differentiated cells have different structures because they express different parts of a largely shared genome. D2.2 explains how those differences arise and persist.
- **C2.1 Chemical signalling** — hormones change cell behavior partly by regulating gene expression. Receptor specificity explains why the same signal doesn’t affect every cell.
- **D2.1 Cell and nuclear division** — epigenetic tags copied during mitosis can preserve a differentiated cell’s pattern of gene expression in its daughter cells.
- **D3.2 Inheritance** — genomic imprinting makes the expression of an allele depend on which parent supplied it, adding a parent-of-origin effect to inheritance.
- **D1.3 Mutation and gene editing** — mutations change DNA nucleotide sequences. Epigenetic regulation changes gene expression while leaving those sequences unchanged.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gene expression | Use of information in a gene to produce a functional RNA or protein that affects cell function or phenotype | Replication of the gene before cell division |
| Promoter | A DNA sequence where transcription machinery assembles to begin transcription of a gene | The protein that carries out transcription |
| Enhancer | A regulatory DNA sequence where activator proteins bind to increase transcription | A sequence translated into a stronger protein |
| Transcription factor | A protein that binds a specific DNA sequence and activates or represses transcription | RNA polymerase or an RNA transcript |
| mRNA degradation | Breakdown of mRNA by nucleases, preventing that molecule from undergoing further translation | Destruction of the gene from which the mRNA was transcribed |
| Epigenesis | Development of patterns of cell differentiation through changes in gene expression | A change in the DNA base sequence |
| Epigenetic tag | A chemical modification of DNA or histones that affects gene expression | An extra nucleotide added to a gene |
| Genome | The complete genetic information of a cell or organism | Only the genes currently being expressed |
| Transcriptome | All RNA transcripts present in a cell or organism at a particular time | All the DNA that could be transcribed |
| Proteome | All proteins present or produced in a cell or organism at a particular time | The amino acid sequence of one protein |
| DNA methylation | Addition of methyl groups to DNA; methylation of cytosine in a promoter represses transcription | A mutation that replaces one DNA base with another |
| Histone methylation | Addition of methyl groups to amino acids in histones, which can activate or repress transcription | A change that always switches a gene off |
| Epigenetic inheritance | Transmission of a pattern of gene expression through retained epigenetic tags | Inheritance caused by a changed nucleotide sequence |
| Genomic imprinting | Parent-of-origin-dependent gene expression caused by epigenetic tags retained on particular alleles | Blending the two parental alleles into one sequence |
| Monozygotic twins | Twins formed from one zygote and therefore having nearly identical genomes | Twins guaranteed to have identical gene expression and phenotypes |
| Operon | A group of bacterial genes regulated together and transcribed from shared regulatory DNA | A group of unrelated proteins joined into one enzyme |
