# A3.1 Diversity of organisms — where the marks go

## Treating similar appearance as proof of one species

**Commonly written:** “These organisms look alike, so they belong to the same species.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Similar morphology supports a classification but doesn’t establish reproductive compatibility; different species can resemble one another.

**Scores instead:** “The organisms fit the same morphological species group, but evidence that they interbreed and produce fertile offspring would be needed for the biological species concept.”

## Leaving fertility out of the biological species concept

**Commonly written:** “Members of the same species can mate and produce offspring.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Closely related species can produce hybrids; the biological species concept requires offspring that are themselves fertile.

**Scores instead:** “A biological species is a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.”

## Formatting both words of a binomial name alike

**Commonly written:** “The scientific name of humans is Homo Sapiens.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The genus begins with a capital letter, but the specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter; both words also need italics or separate underlining.

**Scores instead:** “The human binomial is Homo sapiens, with a capitalized genus, lowercase specific epithet and both words italicized.”

## Swapping karyotype and karyogram

**Commonly written:** “A karyotype is a photograph of chromosomes arranged in pairs.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The arranged image is a karyogram; the karyotype is the chromosome number and appearance shown by that image.

**Scores instead:** “A karyogram displays arranged chromosome images, while a karyotype describes their number and appearance.”

## Saying chromosome 2 proves humans came from chimpanzees

**Commonly written:** “Human chromosome 2 fused after humans evolved from chimpanzees.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Humans and chimpanzees share an ancestor; modern chimpanzees weren’t the ancestors in which the human-lineage fusion occurred.

**Scores instead:** “Human chromosome 2 arose by fusion in the human lineage after humans and chimpanzees diverged from a shared ancestor.”

## Equating more DNA with greater complexity

**Commonly written:** “The species with the largest genome is the most complex because it has the most genes.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Genome size includes non-coding and repetitive DNA, so a larger genome doesn’t necessarily contain more genes or indicate greater complexity.

**Scores instead:** “Genome size measures total DNA and isn’t a reliable indicator of gene number or organism complexity.”

## Calling a dichotomous key an evolutionary tree

**Commonly written:** “A dichotomous key shows which species share the most recent common ancestor.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A key follows paired trait choices to identify a specimen; evolutionary relationships are represented using cladograms or phylogenetic trees.

**Scores instead:** “A dichotomous key identifies a specimen through a sequence of two contrasting statements about observable traits.”

## Treating environmental DNA as a population count

**Commonly written:** “More environmental DNA proves that more living individuals of the species are in the lake.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** DNA can persist after death, move through the habitat and be shed at different rates, so detection alone doesn’t give a reliable population size.

**Scores instead:** “An environmental DNA match shows that the species contributed DNA to the sample, but it doesn’t by itself establish abundance or whether the source was alive.”
