# A3.2 Classification and cladistics — where the marks go

## Treating taxonomic ranks as stages of evolution

**Commonly written:** “Species evolve into genera, then families, orders and classes.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The ranks are nested classification categories, not stages in an evolutionary sequence. Evolution produces branching lineages.

**Scores instead:** “The fixed ranks group organisms at different levels, but the branching pattern of common ancestry doesn’t always fit those arbitrary levels.”

## Leaving descendants out of a clade

**Commonly written:** “A clade is any group of organisms descended from the same ancestor.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A clade must include the common ancestor and all of its descendants, not a selected subset.

**Scores instead:** “A clade contains a common ancestor together with every descendant of that ancestor.”

## Reading relatedness from neighboring names

**Commonly written:** “These species are closest relatives because their names are beside each other on the cladogram.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Page position can change when branches rotate. Relatedness depends on which pair shares the most recent node.

**Scores instead:** “These species are closest relatives because their branches meet at the most recent common ancestor.”

## Reading ordinary branch length as elapsed time

**Commonly written:** “This species evolved for longer because its branch is longer.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Branch length has no quantitative meaning unless the diagram includes a scale or states that length represents time or change.

**Scores instead:** “The sequence of nodes shows the order of divergence, but unscaled branch length can’t be used to calculate time.”

## Trusting similar appearance without checking ancestry

**Commonly written:** “The similar structures prove that the organisms belong in the same clade.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Similar features can evolve independently through convergent evolution and may be analogous rather than inherited from a common ancestor.

**Scores instead:** “Morphology can support a clade, but molecular sequences provide more objective evidence of common ancestry.”

## Treating a molecular-clock date as exact

**Commonly written:** “Twenty sequence differences mean the clades separated exactly ten million years ago.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The calculation relies on an estimated rate that can vary with generation time, population size and selection pressure.

**Scores instead:** “Sequence differences and an estimated mutation rate give an approximate divergence time, not an exact date.”

## Saying parsimony proves a cladogram

**Commonly written:** “The cladogram with the fewest changes is proven to be the true evolutionary history.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Parsimony selects the most probable hypothesis under one criterion; another dataset or criterion can support a different tree.

**Scores instead:** “Parsimony favors the cladogram that accounts for the observed variation with the smallest number of sequence changes.”

## Putting all prokaryotes in one domain

**Commonly written:** “The two domains are Prokaryota and Eukaryota.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** rRNA evidence divided prokaryotic organisms into two distinct domains, producing three domains in total.

**Scores instead:** “The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, with domain ranked above kingdom.”
