# B3.2 Transport — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Explain how a capillary's branching, narrow lumen, one-cell-thick wall and fenestrations help substances move between blood and tissues or exchange surfaces.
- [ ] Compare arteries and veins by wall thickness, lumen size, muscle, elastic fibers and valves, then identify each vessel in a micrograph.
- [ ] Explain how artery walls withstand and maintain high pressure, and how valves, flexible walls and skeletal muscle contractions return venous blood to the heart.
- [ ] Measure pulse rate in beats per minute using manual or electronic methods, and distinguish the pulse from the heartbeat that produces it.
- [ ] Explain how a coronary artery can become occluded, what happens to cardiac muscle downstream, and why a correlation with a risk factor doesn't prove causation.
- [ ] Explain the complete transpiration-cohesion-tension mechanism, including evaporation from leaf cell walls, capillary action, negative pressure and cohesion.
- [ ] Explain how the hollow lumen, absent or perforated end walls, lignin and pits of xylem vessels support water transport under tension.
- [ ] Draw and annotate plan diagrams of dicotyledonous stem and root cross sections, placing epidermis, cortex, vascular tissue, xylem, phloem, cambium and pith where appropriate.

## Higher level

- [ ] Explain how hydrostatic and osmotic pressure differences cause tissue fluid to leave and re-enter capillaries, and compare tissue fluid with plasma.
- [ ] Explain how oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and other substances move between capillaries, tissue fluid and cells.
- [ ] Explain why excess tissue fluid must enter lymph vessels and how lymph is returned to the blood.
- [ ] Compare the single circulation of a bony fish with the double circulation of a mammal, including the pressure consequences of each arrangement.
- [ ] Explain how chambers, cardiac muscle, valves, the septum and coronary vessels adapt the mammalian heart to deliver pressurized blood.
- [ ] Trace the cardiac cycle through atrial systole, ventricular systole and diastole, linking electrical conduction, pressure changes, valve movements and blood flow.
- [ ] Explain how active transport of mineral ions generates root pressure when transpiration is low.
- [ ] Explain translocation from source to sink using the adaptations of sieve tube elements and companion cells and the pressure-flow mechanism.

## Links to other topics

- **A1.1 Water** — cohesion, adhesion and capillary action provide the molecular basis of the transpiration stream.
- **B2.1 Membranes and membrane transport** — osmosis, diffusion and active transport explain tissue-fluid exchange, root pressure and phloem loading.
- **B2.3 Cell specialization** — vessel elements, sieve tube elements, companion cells and cardiac muscle cells show how cell structure fits function.
- **B3.1 Gas exchange** — capillaries carry gases to and from alveoli or gills, connecting exchange surfaces to the rest of the organism.
- **C2.2 Neural signaling** — electrical conduction in the heart uses excitable cells, although the heartbeat begins in cardiac pacemaker cells rather than a motor neuron.
- **C3.1 Integration of body systems** — circulation connects gas exchange, digestion, excretion and cell metabolism.
- **D2.3 Water potential** — differences in water potential explain osmosis into xylem during root pressure and into phloem after sucrose loading.
- **D3.3 Homeostasis** — changing heart rate and vessel diameter helps maintain delivery of oxygen and regulation of internal conditions.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artery | A vessel that carries blood away from the heart | A vessel defined by carrying oxygenated blood; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood |
| Vein | A vessel that carries blood toward the heart | A vessel defined by carrying deoxygenated blood; pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood |
| Capillary | A microscopic exchange vessel with an endothelial wall one cell thick | A small artery with a muscular wall |
| Lumen | The space inside a vessel through which fluid flows | The vessel wall |
| Pulse | A pressure wave caused by ventricular contraction and arterial wall expansion | Blood cells moving as separate waves through an artery |
| Occlusion | A blockage that prevents or greatly reduces flow through a vessel | Any increase in heart rate |
| Transpiration | Loss of water vapor from a plant, mainly through stomata | Active pumping of water up the xylem |
| Transpiration pull | Tension generated when evaporation draws water from xylem into leaf tissues | A force generated by cohesion alone |
| Xylem vessel | A tube of dead, lignified cells that transports water and mineral ions | Living tissue that transports sucrose |
| Tissue fluid | Fluid formed from plasma that surrounds and exchanges substances with cells | Whole blood outside a capillary |
| Lymph | Excess tissue fluid after it enters lymphatic vessels | Plasma that still contains red blood cells |
| Single circulation | A system in which blood passes through the heart once per complete circuit | A heart with only one chamber |
| Double circulation | A system in which blood passes through the heart twice per complete circuit | Two separate supplies of blood that never connect |
| Systole | Contraction of a heart chamber | A term applying only to the ventricles |
| Diastole | Relaxation of a heart chamber | A period when all blood flow stops |
| Root pressure | Positive pressure in xylem produced after ions are transported into it and water enters by osmosis | The main force lifting water through a tall plant during rapid transpiration |
| Translocation | Transport of organic solutes in phloem from sources to sinks | Transport of water and mineral ions in xylem |
| Source | Plant tissue that loads organic solutes into phloem | Always a leaf; storage organs can become sources |
| Sink | Plant tissue that unloads or uses organic solutes from phloem | Always a root; growing leaves, fruits and seeds can be sinks |
