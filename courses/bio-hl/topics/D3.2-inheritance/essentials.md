# D3.2 Inheritance — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Explain how meiosis produces haploid gametes and fertilization combines their alleles in a diploid zygote.
- [ ] Outline how genetic crosses are carried out in flowering plants, using P, F1 and F2 generations and Punnett grids correctly.
- [ ] Use allele symbols and Punnett grids to predict genotypes and phenotypes, while distinguishing genes, alleles, homozygous, heterozygous, dominant and recessive.
- [ ] Explain how genotype and environmental factors interact to produce phenotype, including phenotypic plasticity through changed gene expression.
- [ ] Explain the recessive inheritance and biochemical cause of phenylketonuria.
- [ ] Distinguish a single-nucleotide polymorphism from an allele, explain why a gene pool can contain multiple alleles, and solve ABO blood-group crosses.
- [ ] Distinguish complete dominance, incomplete dominance and codominance from the phenotype of a heterozygote.
- [ ] Explain human sex determination and solve crosses involving X-linked haemophilia using alleles written as superscripts on X chromosomes.
- [ ] Use a pedigree to test possible inheritance patterns, deduce genotypes and explain the increased risk associated with reproduction between close relatives.
- [ ] Distinguish continuous from discrete variation, connect continuous variation to polygenic inheritance and environmental factors, and interpret mean, median and mode.
- [ ] Construct and interpret a box-and-whisker plot, including quartiles, interquartile range and the 1.5 × IQR rule for outliers.

## Higher level

- [ ] Explain how segregation and independent assortment arise from chromosome movements during meiosis.
- [ ] Construct dihybrid Punnett grids for unlinked autosomal genes and derive 9:3:3:1 and 1:1:1:1 phenotypic ratios.
- [ ] Use a gene database to identify human gene loci and polypeptide products, including genes on different chromosomes and linked genes close together on one chromosome.
- [ ] Explain autosomal linkage and crossing over, use chromosome-based linkage notation, and identify parental and recombinant classes in a test cross.
- [ ] Use a chi-squared test to compare observed and expected dihybrid results and make a conclusion at the p = 0.05 significance level.

## Links to other topics

- **D2.1 Cell and nuclear division** — meiosis supplies the chromosome movements behind segregation, independent assortment and crossing over. That turns genetic ratios into consequences rather than rules to memorize.
- **D1.2 Protein synthesis** — alleles can have different base sequences and therefore different polypeptide products. This connection explains how genotype can affect phenotype.
- **D1.3 Mutations and gene editing** — mutation creates new alleles and SNPs. PKU shows how an altered allele can change an enzyme and produce a phenotype.
- **D3.1 Reproduction** — gamete production, pollination and fertilization provide the biological events represented by genetic crosses.
- **D4.1 Natural selection** — inheritance passes heritable variation between generations, while gene pools contain the alleles whose frequencies can change through selection.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gene | A heritable DNA sequence that influences a characteristic, often by coding for a polypeptide or functional RNA | An alternative version of that sequence; that’s an allele |
| Allele | One of the alternative forms of a gene found at the same locus | A whole chromosome or a visible characteristic |
| Locus | The specific position of a gene on a chromosome | The allele occupying that position |
| Genotype | The combination of alleles inherited by an organism | The observable result of those alleles |
| Phenotype | An organism’s observable traits resulting from genotype, environment and their interaction | A direct copy of genotype unaffected by the environment |
| Homozygous | Having two identical alleles of a gene | Having two dominant alleles specifically |
| Heterozygous | Having two different alleles of a gene | Having an intermediate phenotype in every inheritance pattern |
| Dominant allele | An allele that affects the phenotype in a heterozygote | The most common, strongest or most beneficial allele |
| Recessive allele | An allele whose phenotype is masked by a dominant allele in a heterozygote | An allele that disappears or isn’t inherited |
| Codominance | Both alleles have distinct effects in a heterozygote, producing a dual phenotype | Blending into an intermediate phenotype |
| Incomplete dominance | The heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes | Expression of both homozygous phenotypes side by side |
| Phenotypic plasticity | The capacity of one genotype to produce different phenotypes through altered gene expression in different environments | A change in DNA sequence or genotype |
| SNP | Variation at one nucleotide position among individuals | Any large deletion, insertion or chromosome mutation |
| Polygenic inheritance | Inheritance in which multiple genes contribute to one characteristic | Multiple alleles of one gene |
| Sex-linked gene | A gene located on a sex chromosome | Any gene affecting a trait associated with one sex |
| Linked genes | Genes close enough together on the same chromosome to tend to be inherited together | Alleles of the same gene at one locus |
| Recombinant | A gamete or offspring with a combination of alleles different from the combinations in the parent’s homologous chromosomes | Any heterozygous individual |
| Null hypothesis | The prediction that there’s no significant difference between observed and expected results | A claim that the observed and expected values must be identical |
