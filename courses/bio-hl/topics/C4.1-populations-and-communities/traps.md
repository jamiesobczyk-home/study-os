# C4.1 Populations and communities — where the marks go

## Including abiotic factors in the community

**Commonly written:** “A community is all the organisms and environmental conditions in an ecosystem.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A community contains interacting organisms and populations. Abiotic conditions belong to the ecosystem but aren’t members of its community.

**Scores instead:** “A community consists of all the interacting organisms and populations in an ecosystem.”

## Calling convenient quadrats random

**Commonly written:** “Place quadrats where the plants are visible so enough organisms are counted.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Choosing conspicuous patches systematically favors occupied areas and biases the estimate upward. Random locations must be selected before abundance is known.

**Scores instead:** “Generate random coordinates within the study area and place each quadrat at the selected location.”

## Reversing the Lincoln index

**Commonly written:** “Divide the number of marked beetles recaptured by the size of the second sample to estimate the population.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** That calculation gives the marked proportion of the second sample, not population size. The initial marked sample must be divided by that proportion.

**Scores instead:** “Estimate population size as the number initially marked multiplied by the second sample size, divided by the marked number recaptured.”

## Treating carrying capacity as a permanent ceiling

**Commonly written:** “Carrying capacity is the maximum number a habitat can ever contain.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Carrying capacity is the population supported sustainably under current conditions. It changes when resources or environmental conditions change.

**Scores instead:** “Carrying capacity is the population size that an environment can support sustainably under its current conditions.”

## Making every limiting factor density-dependent

**Commonly written:** “A wildfire is density-dependent because it kills more organisms when the population is large.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The number killed may be larger, but density dependence concerns the effect per individual changing with density. Fire can affect individuals regardless of density.

**Scores instead:** “Competition and pathogen transmission are density-dependent, while a wildfire is usually density-independent.”

## Claiming that association proves competition

**Commonly written:** “The significant chi-squared result proves that the two species compete.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Chi-squared shows that the distributions are associated. Shared habitat preferences, avoidance of different conditions or another interaction could produce the pattern.

**Scores instead:** “The significant association is consistent with competition, but a controlled or removal experiment is needed to test the cause.”

## Making predator and prey peaks simultaneous

**Commonly written:** “Lynx numbers peak at the same time as hare numbers because there is more food.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Predator survival and reproduction take time to respond, so the predator peak usually follows the prey peak rather than occurring simultaneously.

**Scores instead:** “A rise in hare numbers is followed after a delay by a rise in lynx numbers because food availability has increased.”

## Reversing top-down and bottom-up control

**Commonly written:** “More plants causing more herbivores is top-down control because the effect increases the food chain.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A change beginning with resource supply at a lower trophic level is bottom-up control. Top-down control begins with consumers above.

**Scores instead:** “More producer biomass supporting more herbivores is bottom-up control, while predators limiting herbivores is top-down control.”
