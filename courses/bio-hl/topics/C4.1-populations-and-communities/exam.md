# C4.1 Populations and communities — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Outline how random quadrat sampling can be used to estimate the size of a plant population. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Define the study area and select quadrat coordinates using a random method. **[1]**
- Place quadrats at the selected coordinates and count individuals using a consistent counting rule. **[1]**
- Calculate the mean number of individuals per quadrat / population density. **[1]**
- Multiply the mean by the number of quadrat-sized areas in the whole study area / multiply density by total area. **[1]**

_Examiner note: throwing quadrats without choosing coordinates isn’t random sampling. Full marks require both random placement and scaling the sample mean to the total area._

## Q2. Calculate and evaluate a population estimate obtained by capture–mark–release–recapture. **[4]**

Eighty beetles were captured, marked and released. In a second sample of 60 beetles, 12 were marked. Calculate the Lincoln index estimate and state two assumptions of the method.

### Mark scheme

- Population estimate = 80 × 60 ÷ 12. **[1]**
- Population estimate = 400 beetles. **[1]**
- Marking does not affect survival / behavior / probability of recapture and marks remain visible. **[1]**
- Marked beetles mix fully with the population / each beetle has an equal probability of capture / population remains closed between samples. **[1]**

_Examiner note: 80 × 60 gives the numerator, but dividing by all 60 beetles instead of the 12 marked recaptures returns the original sample and shows that the marked proportion hasn’t been used._

## Q3. Explain the shape of a sigmoid population-growth curve. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Population growth is initially exponential / the rate of increase per individual is approximately constant. **[1]**
- Resources are initially abundant / density-dependent limiting factors have little effect at low density. **[1]**
- Increasing density causes greater competition for limited resources. **[1]**
- Pathogen transmission / pest effects / predation may increase as density rises. **[1]**
- Birth rate plus immigration becomes approximately equal to death rate plus emigration near carrying capacity / net growth approaches zero. **[1]**

_Examiner note: describing an S shape earns little without its cause. Full marks connect rising density to stronger limiting factors and then to a lower net growth rate._

## Q4. Explain two examples of mutualism required in C4.1. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

Award a maximum of three marks for each example:

- In legume root nodules, bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen compounds available to the plant. **[1]**
- The plant supplies organic compounds / energy to the bacteria. **[1]**
- The plant provides the bacteria with a protected habitat in root nodules. **[1]**
- In orchid mycorrhizae, the fungus supplies water / mineral ions / carbon compounds needed during seed germination. **[1]**
- A photosynthetic orchid can supply organic compounds to the fungus. **[1]**
- Fungal hyphae increase the effective absorptive area available to the orchid. **[1]**
- Zooxanthellae supply hard corals with products of photosynthesis. **[1]**
- Corals supply zooxanthellae with carbon dioxide / mineral nutrients. **[1]**
- Corals provide zooxanthellae with a protected, well-lit position. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming two partners and saying that they “help each other” doesn’t explain mutualism. The answer must identify a benefit received by each partner in both examples._

## Q5. Suggest how presence-or-absence data and a chi-squared test could provide evidence of competition between two plant species. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Record presence or absence of both species at many randomly selected sampling sites / quadrats. **[1]**
- Use a contingency table and a null hypothesis that the distributions of the species are independent. **[1]**
- A significant chi-squared result allows rejection of the null hypothesis / supports an association between distributions. **[1]**
- The association indicates but does not prove competition because abiotic preferences / another species / another interaction could cause the pattern. **[1]**

_Examiner note: chi-squared tests association, not the biological mechanism behind it. Claiming that a significant result proves competition loses the evaluation mark._

## Q6. Compare and contrast top-down and bottom-up control of populations. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Both can regulate population sizes within a community / produce changes across trophic levels. **[1]**
- Top-down control begins with consumers at higher trophic levels affecting populations below them. **[1]**
- Accept: increased predator abundance can reduce prey abundance / a predator-removal example. **[1]**
- Bottom-up control begins with resource availability at lower trophic levels affecting populations above them. **[1]**
- Accept: increased producer biomass can support more herbivores and predators / reduced nutrient supply can limit higher trophic levels. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “top-down starts at the top” only labels the idea. Full marks require the direction of the effect and a population consequence for each type of control._
