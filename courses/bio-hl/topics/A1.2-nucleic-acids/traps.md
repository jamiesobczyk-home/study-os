# A1.2 Nucleic acids — where the marks go

## Calling a base a nucleotide

**Commonly written:** “Adenine is a nucleotide in DNA.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Adenine is one nitrogenous base. A nucleotide also needs a pentose sugar and a phosphate group.

**Scores instead:** “An adenine nucleotide contains the base adenine attached to a pentose sugar and a phosphate group.”

## Putting hydrogen bonds in the backbone

**Commonly written:** “Hydrogen bonds join the sugars and phosphates along a DNA strand.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Sugar and phosphate are covalently linked within one strand. Hydrogen bonds join complementary bases across two strands.

**Scores instead:** “Covalent phosphodiester bonds form each sugar–phosphate backbone, while hydrogen bonds join complementary bases between strands.”

## Drawing both strands in the same direction

**Commonly written:** “Both DNA strands run 5′ to 3′ from left to right.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** The strands are antiparallel. If one runs 5′ to 3′ left to right, its partner runs 3′ to 5′ left to right.

**Scores instead:** “The two DNA strands are antiparallel, so their 5′ to 3′ directions are opposite.”

## Pairing A with U in DNA

**Commonly written:** “Adenine pairs with uracil in the DNA double helix.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** DNA uses thymine opposite adenine. Uracil replaces thymine in RNA, including the RNA copied from a DNA template.

**Scores instead:** “Adenine pairs with thymine in DNA, while adenine pairs with uracil in RNA.”

## Confusing code with base sequence

**Commonly written:** “All organisms have the same DNA sequence because the genetic code is universal.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Organisms differ in DNA sequence. Near-universality concerns the mapping from mRNA codons to amino acids, not matching genomes.

**Scores instead:** “Nearly all organisms use the same correspondence between mRNA codons and amino acids despite having different DNA sequences.”

## Assuming all four bases have equal percentages

**Commonly written:** “Chargaff proved that A, T, G and C each make up 25% of DNA.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A is approximately equal to T and G to C within double-stranded DNA, but A+T and G+C proportions can vary across species.

**Scores instead:** “Chargaff found that A≈T and G≈C within species, while the overall base composition varied between species.”

## Swapping the Hershey–Chase isotopes

**Commonly written:** “Sulfur-35 labeled DNA and entered the bacteria.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Sulfur-35 labeled phage protein, mostly found outside the bacteria. Phosphorus-32 labeled DNA and was found with the bacterial pellet.

**Scores instead:** “Phosphorus-32-labeled DNA was found mainly with the infected bacterial pellet, while sulfur-35-labeled protein remained mainly in the supernatant.”

## Treating the nucleosome as one histone

**Commonly written:** “DNA sits inside a single histone to make a nucleosome.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** DNA wraps around a core of eight histones, with linker DNA between cores and an associated linker histone.

**Scores instead:** “A nucleosome has DNA wrapped around an eight-histone core, with linker DNA connecting it to neighboring nucleosomes.”
