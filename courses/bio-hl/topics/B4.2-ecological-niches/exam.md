# B4.2 Ecological niches — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Explain the difference between the fundamental and realized niche of a species. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Fundamental niche is the potential niche of a species based on its adaptations and tolerance limits. **[1]**
- Realized niche is the actual extent of the niche occupied when other species are present. **[1]**
- Interspecific competition can prevent a species from using part of its fundamental niche. **[1]**
- The realized niche may therefore be narrower / restricted compared with the fundamental niche. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “One is possible and one is actual” earns limited credit unless the answer connects the fundamental niche to adaptations and tolerance limits, and the realized niche to competition._

## Q2. Distinguish the oxygen requirements of obligate anaerobes, facultative anaerobes and obligate aerobes. **[3]**

### Mark scheme

- Obligate anaerobes grow only in the absence of oxygen / cannot tolerate oxygen in their environment. **[1]**
- Facultative anaerobes can grow in either the presence or absence of oxygen. **[1]**
- Obligate aerobes require oxygen in their environment. **[1]**

_Examiner note: descriptions of aerobic and anaerobic respiration don’t answer this question by themselves. The command term asks for differences in tolerance of environmental oxygen._

## Q3. Explain saprotrophic nutrition and distinguish it from holozoic nutrition. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Saprotrophs secrete digestive enzymes onto dead organic matter. **[1]**
- Digestion by saprotrophs occurs externally / outside the organism. **[1]**
- Soluble products of external digestion are absorbed. **[1]**
- Holozoic nutrition involves ingestion followed by internal digestion. **[1]**
- Products of holozoic digestion are absorbed and assimilated. **[1]**

_Examiner note: both modes are heterotrophic, so saying that they “consume other organisms” doesn’t distinguish them. The location of digestion and the presence or absence of ingestion do._

## Q4. Suggest how skull evidence could be used to infer that an extinct hominid ate a large amount of tough plant material. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

Award any four:

- Large premolars and/or molars provide broad surfaces for crushing and grinding. **[1]**
- Thick enamel resists wear caused by prolonged chewing / abrasive food. **[1]**
- A robust jaw withstands large forces during chewing. **[1]**
- Large attachment areas for chewing muscles indicate the production of strong bite forces. **[1]**
- The inference should be based on a combination of anatomical features / comparison with living mammals of known diet. **[1]**

_Examiner note: naming a large jaw without explaining its relationship to processing food is description, not a complete dietary inference._

## Q5. Compare adaptations of a leaf-eating insect and a plant involved in their feeding relationship. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

Award any six:

- Piercing mouthparts allow an insect to penetrate plant tissues and withdraw fluids. **[1]**
- Chewing mouthparts allow an insect to cut / crush leaf material before ingestion. **[1]**
- Thorns / spines / tough physical structures make plant tissue harder or more dangerous to eat. **[1]**
- Toxic secondary compounds in seeds or leaves deter feeding / reduce herbivore survival. **[1]**
- Some herbivores possess metabolic adaptations that detoxify plant toxins. **[1]**
- Herbivore feeding adaptations increase access to plant nutrients, whereas plant defenses reduce damage from feeding. **[1]**
- Adaptation by either species can impose selection pressure on the other species. **[1]**

_Examiner note: a list of structures can score some marks, but full marks require functions and a comparison between adaptations that promote feeding and adaptations that resist it._

## Q6. Explain why competition between two species with very similar niches may change the community. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- The species use the same limiting resource(s) / have strongly overlapping niches. **[1]**
- Interspecific competition reduces access to the resource for one or both species. **[1]**
- One species may be the superior competitor and eliminate the other species locally. **[1]**
- Alternatively, both species may become restricted to different parts of their fundamental niches. **[1]**
- The resulting realized niches are more distinct / the abundance or distribution of the species changes. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “the strongest survives” misses both the shared limiting resource and the alternative outcome in which both species remain but occupy restricted realized niches._
