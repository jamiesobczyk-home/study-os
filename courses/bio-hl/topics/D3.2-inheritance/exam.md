# D3.2 Inheritance — exam practice

> Write each answer before revealing the mark scheme. A point that looked familiar after the reveal wasn’t recalled and didn’t score.

## Q1. Explain how meiosis and fertilization produce inheritance in a sexually reproducing eukaryote. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- Meiosis produces haploid gametes / halves the chromosome number. **[1]**
- Homologous chromosomes separate, so each gamete receives one allele of each autosomal gene. **[1]**
- Two haploid gametes fuse during fertilization. **[1]**
- A diploid zygote is formed with one copy of each autosomal gene from each parent. **[1]**

_Examiner note: saying that offspring receive DNA from both parents earns little without the halving and restoration of chromosome number._

## Q2. Distinguish complete dominance, incomplete dominance and codominance. **[4]**

### Mark scheme

- In complete dominance, the heterozygote has the same phenotype as the homozygous dominant individual. **[1]**
- In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. **[1]**
- Accept: red and white four o’clock flower alleles produce a pink heterozygote. **[1]**
- In codominance, both alleles have distinct phenotypic effects in the heterozygote / Accept: I^AI^B produces blood group AB. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “both alleles are expressed” describes codominance, not incomplete dominance. The intermediate phenotype is the required distinction._

## Q3. Explain the inheritance of haemophilia when a carrier mother has children with an unaffected father. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Haemophilia is caused by a recessive allele carried on the X chromosome. **[1]**
- Mother’s genotype is X^HX^h and father’s genotype is X^HY, with allele symbols shown as superscripts. **[1]**
- Possible offspring are X^HX^H, X^HX^h, X^HY and X^hY in equal predicted proportions. **[1]**
- Half the sons are predicted to have haemophilia, while no daughters are predicted to have haemophilia. **[1]**
- A father passes his Y chromosome, rather than his X chromosome, to each son / there’s no father-to-son transmission of the X-linked allele. **[1]**

_Examiner note: a percentage without identifying whether it applies to all children or to sons loses precision. In this cross, 25% of all children but 50% of sons are predicted to be affected._

## Q4. Explain how a pedigree can support autosomal recessive inheritance and why reproduction between close relatives increases the risk of a recessive disorder. **[5]**

### Mark scheme

- Two unaffected parents may have an affected child if both are heterozygous carriers. **[1]**
- An affected individual has two recessive alleles / is homozygous recessive. **[1]**
- The condition may skip generations and can occur in individuals of either sex. **[1]**
- Close relatives have a higher probability of sharing an allele inherited from a recent common ancestor. **[1]**
- They therefore have a higher probability of producing a child homozygous for the same rare recessive allele. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “close relatives have similar DNA” is too broad. The scoring link is shared ancestry, the same recessive allele and increased homozygosity in an offspring._

## Q5. Explain the expected results of a test cross between AaBb and aabb, first for unlinked genes and then for linked genes in the arrangement AB/ab. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- For unlinked genes, AaBb produces AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes in equal proportions due to independent assortment. **[1]**
- The aabb parent produces only ab gametes. **[1]**
- The expected unlinked phenotypic ratio is 1:1:1:1. **[1]**
- For linked genes in AB/ab, AB and ab are the parental gametes / allele combinations. **[1]**
- Ab and aB are recombinant gametes produced by crossing over between the loci. **[1]**
- Parental offspring classes are expected to be more frequent than recombinant classes. **[1]**

_Examiner note: four gamete types alone don’t show independent assortment. Full marks require equal frequencies for unlinked genes and unequal parental versus recombinant frequencies for linked genes._

## Q6. A dihybrid cross produces four observed phenotype classes. Explain how a chi-squared test is used to test the hypothesis that the genes assort independently. **[6]**

### Mark scheme

- State the null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed numbers and numbers expected from independent assortment. **[1]**
- Calculate expected numbers from the appropriate predicted ratio and sample total. **[1]**
- Calculate χ² using Σ((O − E)²/E). **[1]**
- Use three degrees of freedom for four phenotype categories / degrees of freedom equal categories minus one. **[1]**
- Compare the calculated χ² with the critical value at p = 0.05. **[1]**
- Reject the null hypothesis if χ² exceeds the critical value / p < 0.05; otherwise fail to reject it. **[1]**

_Examiner note: “accept the null hypothesis” is too strong. A non-significant result means the data don’t provide enough evidence to reject it, not that independent assortment has been proved._
