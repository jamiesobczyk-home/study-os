# A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity — where the marks go

## Defining biodiversity as the number of species

**Commonly written:** “Biodiversity is how many species live in an area.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** That defines species richness only. Biodiversity also includes genetic diversity, relative species abundance and ecosystem diversity.

**Scores instead:** “Biodiversity is the variety of life at genetic, species and ecosystem levels and in combinations of those levels.”

## Using high present biodiversity to deny the crisis

**Commonly written:** “There are more species now than in the past, so there can’t be a biodiversity crisis.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Present species number and current rate of decline measure different things. High diversity can exist while populations and species are being lost rapidly.

**Scores instead:** “Fossils suggest present diversity may be historically high, while repeated surveys still show current declines in richness, evenness and populations.”

## Giving a cause as evidence of change

**Commonly written:** “Deforestation proves that biodiversity has decreased.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Deforestation is a possible cause. Evidence of change requires biodiversity measurements, such as comparable surveys made at different times.

**Scores instead:** “Repeated surveys using comparable methods can show declines in species richness or evenness after deforestation.”

## Treating richness and evenness as the same measure

**Commonly written:** “The community has high evenness because it contains 20 species.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Twenty species describes richness. Evenness depends on how similar the species’ relative abundances are.

**Scores instead:** “The community has a richness of 20 species, while its evenness depends on how evenly individuals are distributed among them.”

## Naming population growth as the direct mechanism

**Commonly written:** “Human population growth directly makes species extinct.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Population growth is an overarching pressure. The missing links are increased demand and the resulting habitat loss, overexploitation, pollution or biological invasions.

**Scores instead:** “Population growth increases demand for land and food, driving agricultural clearance that removes and fragments natural habitat.”

## Calling zoo conservation in situ

**Commonly written:** “A captive-breeding program in a zoo is in situ conservation.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** A zoo is outside the species’ natural habitat, so captive breeding there is ex situ conservation.

**Scores instead:** “Captive breeding in a zoo is ex situ conservation, while protecting the wild population in its habitat is in situ conservation.”

## Treating rewilding and reclamation as interchangeable

**Commonly written:** “Rewilding means repairing polluted soil and planting an entire replacement habitat.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Intensive repair of a degraded site is reclamation. Rewilding focuses on restoring natural processes and reducing continued human control.

**Scores instead:** “Reclamation repairs degraded habitat, while rewilding restores natural ecological processes, sometimes by reintroducing native species.”

## Prioritizing EDGE species by extinction risk alone

**Commonly written:** “EDGE selects whichever species is most endangered.”

**Why it doesn’t score:** Global endangerment is only one criterion. EDGE also considers how much unique evolutionary history the species represents.

**Scores instead:** “EDGE prioritizes species that are both globally endangered and evolutionarily distinct.”
