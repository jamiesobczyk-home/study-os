# C4.1 Populations and communities — what you need to be able to do

> Tick a line only after you can do it with the page closed and without prompts.

## Core

- [ ] Distinguish a population from a community, including the role of breeding and reproductive isolation in identifying populations.
- [ ] Explain why ecologists estimate population size by random sampling, and identify sampling error as the difference between an estimate and the true population size.
- [ ] Use random quadrats to estimate the population size of a sessile species, and interpret standard deviation as evidence about variation among quadrats.
- [ ] Use capture–mark–release–recapture data and the Lincoln index to estimate the population size of a motile species, stating the method’s assumptions.
- [ ] Define carrying capacity and explain how competition for limited resources restricts population growth.
- [ ] Distinguish density-dependent from density-independent limiting factors and explain how density-dependent factors provide negative feedback.
- [ ] Compare exponential and sigmoid population growth, interpret the curves, and test exponential growth using a logarithmic population axis.
- [ ] Explain why population-growth models are useful but don’t reproduce every fluctuation in a natural population.
- [ ] Describe how competition and cooperation between members of the same species affect survival and reproduction.
- [ ] Classify interspecific relationships as herbivory, predation, competition, mutualism, parasitism or pathogenicity, using examples.
- [ ] Explain what each partner gains in legume root nodules, orchid mycorrhizae and the relationship between hard corals and zooxanthellae.
- [ ] Explain how superior acquisition of a limited resource can allow an introduced species to become invasive and reduce endemic populations.
- [ ] Design or interpret laboratory experiments, random field observations and removal experiments used to test for interspecific competition.
- [ ] Apply a chi-squared test to presence-or-absence data for two species and explain why an association doesn’t prove competition.
- [ ] Explain predator–prey cycles as density-dependent population control, using a real example.
- [ ] Distinguish top-down from bottom-up control and compare allelopathy with antibiotic secretion as chemical interference with competitors.

- **B4.2 Ecological niches** — a niche includes interactions with resources, competitors, predators and mutualists. Those interactions become the population controls studied here.
- **C4.2 Transfers of energy and matter** — feeding relationships transfer energy, while changes in food supply can produce bottom-up control of populations.

## Vocabulary that has to be exact

| Term | What it means | What it’s not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Population | Interacting organisms of the same species living in an area, whose members normally breed with one another | Every organism in the area |
| Community | All the interacting organisms and populations in an ecosystem | The abiotic environment together with the organisms |
| Population size | The number of individuals in a population | Population density, which is number per unit area or volume |
| Random sampling | Selecting sampling locations so each possible location has an equal chance of selection | Placing samples where organisms are easiest to find |
| Sampling error | The difference between a sample-based estimate and the true value for the whole population | A calculation mistake or a biased method |
| Quadrat | A frame defining a known area in which sessile organisms are counted | A suitable method for animals that move rapidly between samples |
| Standard deviation | A measure of how spread out sampled values are around their mean | A measure of whether the mean equals the true population size |
| Lincoln index | An estimate based on the proportion of marked individuals found in a second capture | A direct count of every organism in the population |
| Carrying capacity | The population size an environment can support sustainably under its current conditions | A fixed maximum that can never change |
| Density-dependent factor | A factor whose effect becomes stronger or changes as population density changes | Any factor that happens to reduce population size |
| Density-independent factor | A factor whose effect doesn’t depend on population density, such as a severe weather event | A factor that reliably returns a population to carrying capacity |
| Exponential growth | Growth at an approximately constant rate per individual, producing a J-shaped curve on ordinary axes | Growth by the same number of individuals in every time interval |
| Sigmoid growth | Growth that begins rapidly and then slows near carrying capacity, producing an S-shaped curve | A curve that proves a real population remains exactly at carrying capacity |
| Intraspecific | Occurring between members of the same species | Occurring between different species |
| Interspecific | Occurring between members of different species | Occurring within one population |
| Mutualism | An interspecific relationship in which both species benefit | A relationship in which one benefits and the other is unaffected |
| Allelopathy | Release of chemicals by a plant that inhibit potential competitors | Consumption of another plant by a herbivore |
| Top-down control | Regulation of lower trophic levels by consumers at higher trophic levels | Regulation caused by the supply of resources at lower trophic levels |
| Bottom-up control | Regulation of populations by resource supply from lower trophic levels | Regulation caused by predators at higher trophic levels |
